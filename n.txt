\title[Multimodal Fusion in Deep Learning: A Comprehensive Review of Architectures, Robustness, and Applications]{Multimodal Fusion in Deep Learning: A Comprehensive Review of Architectures, Robustness, and Applications}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Rezaur Rahman} \sur{Ratul}}\email{rratul212025@bscse.uiu.ac.bd}

\abstract{Multimodal fusion enables deep learning systems to integrate complementary information from heterogeneous data sources, yet the growing range of methods, architectures, data conditions, and application settings makes the field difficult to compare systematically. This review presents MULTIFUSION-7, a seven-dimensional taxonomy that organizes multimodal fusion according to fusion stage, fusion mechanism, modality type, learning paradigm, data condition, backbone architecture, and application domain. Using this framework, we examine early, intermediate, late, hybrid, and adaptive fusion, alongside concatenation-, attention-, tensor-, graph-, probabilistic-, contrastive-, gated-, and generative-based approaches. We discuss joint and coordinated representation learning and consider the roles of convolutional and recurrent networks, graph neural networks, Transformers, autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, and diffusion models. Particular attention is given to practical data conditions, including missing, noisy, imbalanced, and asynchronous modalities, which can affect the reliability of fused representations and downstream predictions. The review also summarizes representative evaluation datasets and metrics across healthcare, autonomous driving, robotics, emotion recognition, and remote sensing. Across these topics, recurring challenges include cross-modal alignment, computational efficiency, robustness, generalization, interpretability, and the comparability of evaluation protocols. Finally, we outline research directions in efficient, uncertainty-aware, self-supervised, explainable, federated, and continual multimodal learning. By connecting fusion strategies with their architectural assumptions, data conditions, application contexts, and evaluation practices, MULTIFUSION-7 provides an organizing framework for comparing existing approaches and identifying priorities for future research.}

\keywords{Multimodal fusion, deep learning, fusion taxonomy, representation learning, attention mechanisms, missing modalities, robustness, evaluation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern deep learning systems increasingly operate on data collected from multiple sources, including images, text, audio, video, physiological measurements, and environmental sensors. These sources often describe complementary aspects of the same event or entity but differ in their statistical properties, temporal resolution, representation, and reliability. Multimodal fusion aims to integrate such heterogeneous information so that a model can exploit cross-modal relationships that may be unavailable from any single source. Consequently, it has become an important component of applications such as clinical decision support, autonomous perception, emotion recognition, robotics, and remote sensing. Early surveys established core principles for multimodal fusion, while later work examined the architectures and learning strategies used to learn representations across modalities \citep{atrey2010multimodal,ramachandram2017deep,baltrusaitis2019survey}.

Fusion, however, is not a single operation or a universally applicable design choice. Models may combine inputs at the beginning of a processing pipeline, integrate features within intermediate layers, aggregate modality-specific predictions at the decision stage, or combine several stages in hybrid or adaptive arrangements. These choices influence which cross-modal interactions can be learned, how strongly a system depends on aligned inputs, and how much modality-specific information is retained. The fusion mechanism introduces a further design dimension: simple concatenation, tensor interactions, attention, graph-based reasoning, probabilistic integration, contrastive objectives, and generative approaches represent different assumptions about how information should be related. For example, Tensor Fusion Networks explicitly model intra-modal and inter-modal interactions for multimodal sentiment analysis, whereas the Multimodal Transformer uses cross-modal attention to model unaligned language, audio, and visual sequences \citep{zadeh2017tensor,tsai2019multimodal}. These methods illustrate the diversity of approaches, but they do not imply that one fusion strategy is optimal across tasks and data conditions.

The design space is also shaped by the modalities involved, the learning paradigm, and the backbone architecture. Image--text fusion, audio--visual learning, sensor fusion, and the integration of medical or remote-sensing data impose different requirements for representation, alignment, and interaction. Similarly, convolutional and recurrent networks, graph neural networks, Transformers, autoencoders, and generative models provide different ways to encode modality-specific information and construct joint representations. As a result, comparing methods only by their headline architecture or application domain can obscure important differences in the stage of fusion, the learning objective, the assumptions about data availability, and the evaluation protocol. A clear analytical framework should therefore describe these dimensions together rather than treating them as independent lists of methods.

A further difficulty is that real-world multimodal data are rarely complete and uniformly reliable. Sensors may fail, modalities may be unavailable for some samples, temporal streams may not align, and one modality may dominate learning because it contains stronger or easier-to-optimise signals. Such conditions can undermine a fusion model even when it performs well on fully observed, carefully curated data. Research on ModDrop demonstrated how modality dropout can encourage models to remain useful when some signals are absent, while more recent survey work specifically examines deep multimodal learning with missing modalities \citep{neverova2016moddrop,wu2026missingmodality}. These challenges motivate an assessment of fusion methods that considers not only task performance but also robustness, computational cost, interpretability, and the suitability of evaluation datasets and metrics.

Existing surveys provide essential foundations, but their emphases differ. Atrey et al. organized multimodal fusion methods by methodology and fusion level and Ramachandram and Taylor reviewed deep multimodal architectures and representation fusion and Baltru\v{s}aitis et al. presented a broad taxonomy of multimodal machine learning, including representation, alignment, translation, fusion, and co-learning \citep{atrey2010multimodal,ramachandram2017deep,baltrusaitis2019survey}. More focused reviews address particular application areas or challenges, such as learning with missing modalities \citep{wu2026missingmodality}. Taken together, these works establish a substantial knowledge base, but their different scopes make it useful to have a single organizing view that explicitly connects fusion stage, fusion mechanism, modality type, learning paradigm, data condition, backbone architecture, and application domain. This review uses that need as its starting point and avoids assuming that a taxonomy alone resolves the technical trade-offs among methods.

Accordingly, this paper presents \textit{MULTIFUSION-7}, a seven-dimensional taxonomy for examining multimodal fusion in deep learning. The review focuses on fusion approaches built around general deep learning architectures, including CNNs, RNNs/LSTMs, graph neural networks, Transformers, autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, and diffusion models; it does not aim to provide a general survey of large language models or multimodal foundation models. In addition to organizing methods by the seven dimensions, we discuss fusion architectures and representation-learning strategies, examine robustness under missing, noisy, imbalanced, and asynchronous data conditions, and summarize evaluation practices and applications across domains. The framework is intended to help readers compare methods consistently, identify the assumptions under which they operate, and locate open problems for future research.

The main contributions of this review are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A seven-dimensional taxonomy.} We introduce MULTIFUSION-7 to organize the literature through fusion stage, fusion mechanism, modality, learning paradigm, data condition, backbone architecture, and application domain.
    \item \textbf{A comparative synthesis of fusion designs.} We discuss early, intermediate, late, hybrid, and adaptive fusion alongside attention-based, tensor-based, graph-based, probabilistic, contrastive, and generative approaches, relating these choices to common architectural backbones.
    \item \textbf{A review of representation learning and robustness.} We examine joint and coordinated representations, contrastive learning, and strategies for handling missing, noisy, and imbalanced modalities.
    \item \textbf{An overview of evaluation and applications.} We summarize representative benchmarks and performance measures and discuss multimodal fusion in healthcare, autonomous driving, robotics, emotion recognition, and remote sensing.
    \item \textbf{Open challenges and future directions.} We synthesize recurring limitations involving cross-modal alignment, computational demands, robustness, generalization, and reliable evaluation, and outline directions for further investigation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:relatedworks}

Research on multimodal fusion has been examined through several complementary perspectives, including fusion-level classifications, deep multimodal representation learning, technique-oriented taxonomies, application-specific reviews, and methods to deal with incomplete inputs. These perspectives overlap, but they do not necessarily organize the literature around the same questions. Some reviews primarily describe where fusion occurs in a processing pipeline, whereas others group methods by their underlying architecture or learning mechanism. Domain-specific surveys, in turn, provide detailed coverage of selected modalities and tasks. This section reviews these strands, summarizes representative studies in Table~\ref{tab:related-work-taxonomy}, and explains the scope of the MULTIFUSION-7 taxonomy in relation to previous work.

Foundational Surveys and Conceptual Taxonomies

\subsection{Foundational Surveys and Conceptual Taxonomies}
\label{subsec:foundational_surveys}

The development of multimodal fusion has been accompanied by several
foundational surveys and conceptual taxonomies that organize the field
according to fusion strategies, representation-learning objectives,
architectural designs, and application requirements. These studies
provide complementary perspectives on how heterogeneous information
can be integrated and identify recurring challenges associated with
alignment, information complementarity, modality reliability, and
generalization. Understanding these contributions is essential for
establishing a consistent basis for comparing contemporary deep
multimodal fusion methods
\citep{atrey2010multimodal,ramachandram2017deep,baltrusaitis2019survey}.

One of the early systematic treatments of multimodal fusion was
presented by Atrey et al. \citep{atrey2010multimodal}, who surveyed
fusion methodologies for multimedia analysis and distinguished
feature-level, decision-level, and hybrid fusion. Feature-level fusion
integrates information extracted from multiple sources into a common
feature representation, whereas decision-level fusion combines
modality-specific predictions or decisions. Hybrid fusion incorporates
more than one integration strategy within a single system. Beyond
these categories, the authors discussed practical factors influencing
fusion effectiveness, including inter-modal correlation, confidence,
contextual information, synchronization, and modality selection.
Their analysis established an important conceptual foundation for
understanding fusion as a design problem involving both the location
of integration and the characteristics of the information being
combined. However, this classification predates many contemporary
deep learning architectures in which feature extraction, cross-modal
interaction, and prediction are jointly optimized.

Subsequently, Ramachandram and Taylor
\citep{ramachandram2017deep} reviewed advances in deep multimodal
learning, emphasizing the role of neural architectures in learning
representations from heterogeneous data sources. Their work connected
traditional fusion concepts with learned feature extraction and
discussed the challenges associated with designing models that exploit
relationships across modalities. An important implication of this
perspective is that multimodal fusion cannot be understood solely as
the concatenation or aggregation of independently extracted features.
Instead, the effectiveness of a fusion model also depends on how its
encoders represent modality-specific information, how the representations
interact, and how the learning objective encourages useful cross-modal
relationships. This architectural perspective provides a foundation
for examining convolutional neural networks, recurrent architectures,
and attention-based models within a common multimodal learning
framework.

A broader conceptual taxonomy was introduced by Baltru\v{s}aitis
et al. \citep{baltrusaitis2019survey}, who organized multimodal
machine learning around five central challenges: representation,
translation, alignment, fusion, and co-learning. Representation
concerns how information from different modalities is encoded in
forms suitable for learning. Translation addresses the mapping of
information from one modality to another. Alignment establishes
correspondences between elements or representations across modalities.
Fusion determines how information from multiple sources is integrated,
while co-learning investigates how knowledge obtained from one modality
or learning task can benefit another. These categories describe
interrelated aspects of multimodal learning rather than mutually
exclusive architectural classes.

This distinction is particularly important because successful fusion
depends on more than the integration operator itself. For example,
combining visual and textual features may be ineffective if the
representations do not encode corresponding semantic information.
Similarly, temporal fusion between audio and video may be unreliable
when the streams are not appropriately aligned. The taxonomy of
Baltru\v{s}aitis et al. therefore provides a broader conceptual
framework in which fusion is considered alongside the representation,
mapping, and alignment processes that support it. In contrast to
taxonomies focused primarily on the location of fusion, this framework
helps explain the wider set of learning problems that determine
whether multimodal integration is effective.

As deep learning architectures and applications have expanded,
subsequent surveys have examined the design space from more
architecture-oriented perspectives. Gao et al.
\citep{gao2020survey} reviewed deep learning approaches for multimodal
data fusion, discussing representative models, learning strategies,
and challenges associated with integrating heterogeneous information.
Their work further established the importance of learned representations
and neural architectures in modern fusion systems. More recently,
Jiao et al. \citep{jiao2024comprehensivesurvey} examined deep learning-based
multimodal fusion through fusion stages, representation, mapping,
alignment, applications, and unresolved research challenges. Their
analysis emphasizes that fusion performance depends on the ability
to preserve complementary information while limiting the adverse
effects of redundant, noisy, or missing data.

A further development in this literature is the technique-oriented
taxonomy presented by Zhao et al. \citep{zhao2024deepfusion}. Rather than
relying exclusively on conventional distinctions such as early,
intermediate, and late fusion, their survey groups deep multimodal
fusion approaches into five broad categories: encoder--decoder methods,
attention mechanisms, graph neural network methods, generative neural
network methods, and constraint-based methods. This organization
reflects the increasing integration of feature extraction, cross-modal
interaction, and prediction within individual neural architectures.
It also illustrates why fusion methods cannot always be classified
adequately by identifying the point at which information is combined.
A single architecture may incorporate multiple mechanisms, and the
same mechanism may be used at different stages or under different
learning objectives.

Taken together, these surveys establish complementary foundations
for analyzing multimodal fusion. Atrey et al. emphasize fusion
methodology and integration levels; Ramachandram and Taylor focus
on deep architectures and representation learning; Baltru\v{s}aitis
et al. organize the broader multimodal learning problem; and later
surveys extend the discussion toward modern fusion techniques,
architectural categories, and application-specific requirements
\citep{atrey2010multimodal,ramachandram2017deep,
baltrusaitis2019survey,gao2020survey,
jiao2024comprehensivesurvey,zhao2024deepfusion}. These contributions should be
viewed as complementary rather than competing classifications because
they address different aspects of the same research landscape.

Despite this substantial body of work, comparing multimodal fusion
methods remains challenging when individual studies emphasize
different classification criteria. A method described as
attention-based, for example, may also be characterized by its fusion
stage, backbone architecture, learning paradigm, supported modalities,
and behavior under incomplete or noisy inputs. Conversely, two
methods using the same fusion mechanism may differ substantially
in their assumptions about alignment, modality availability, and
the application for which they were designed. These differences
can make comparisons based on architecture names or reported
predictive performance alone insufficient.

To address this organizational challenge, the present review
introduces MULTIFUSION-7, a seven-dimensional taxonomy that
characterizes multimodal fusion through (i) fusion stage,
(ii) fusion mechanism, (iii) modality type, (iv) learning paradigm,
(v) data condition, (vi) backbone architecture, and
(vii) application domain. The framework does not seek to replace
the established taxonomies described above. Instead, it brings
their complementary perspectives together in a common classification
scheme, enabling each method to be examined in relation to both
its architectural design and its operating conditions. In particular,
the explicit treatment of missing, noisy, imbalanced, and asynchronous
data provides a basis for examining robustness alongside conventional
fusion design choices.

The usefulness of MULTIFUSION-7 therefore lies in its cross-dimensional
organization rather than in the introduction of an entirely new
fusion mechanism. By distinguishing where information is integrated
from how it is integrated, and by considering both dimensions alongside
the learning paradigm, backbone, data condition, modality, and
application, the taxonomy provides a structured foundation for the
comparative analysis developed in the subsequent sections.


\subsection{Recent Surveys of Deep Multimodal Fusion}
\label{subsec:recent_surveys}

Recent surveys have extended the foundations of multimodal learning by
examining contemporary fusion architectures, representation-learning
strategies, modality combinations, and application-specific requirements.
As deep neural networks increasingly integrate feature extraction,
cross-modal interaction, and prediction within unified architectures,
traditional classifications based exclusively on the stage of fusion
have become insufficient to characterize the full design space
\citep{jiao2024comprehensivesurvey,zhao2024deepfusion,
baltrusaitis2019survey}. Recent reviews therefore explore the field
from complementary perspectives, including fusion stages, architectural
mechanisms, self-supervised representation learning, application domains,
and robustness under incomplete or degraded input conditions.

Jiao et al. \citep{jiao2024comprehensivesurvey} presented a comprehensive
survey of deep learning-based multimodal fusion, organizing methods
according to early, deep, late, and hybrid fusion. Their analysis
examines representation learning, mapping, alignment, applications,
datasets, and unresolved challenges. The survey emphasizes that effective
fusion requires more than combining multiple input streams: a model must
preserve complementary information, manage redundant features, and
account for practical difficulties such as missing observations and
measurement noise. This stage-oriented perspective is useful for
understanding where information integration occurs in a processing
pipeline and how the choice of fusion stage influences the architecture.
However, the stage-based classification alone does not fully describe
the range of mechanisms that may operate within a single deep learning
model.

Zhao et al. \citep{zhao2024deepfusion} provided a detailed survey of deep
multimodal data fusion in \textit{ACM Computing Surveys}. They organized
modern approaches into five technique-oriented categories: encoder--decoder
methods, attention mechanisms, graph neural networks, generative neural
networks, and constraint-based methods. Their analysis reflects the
increasing integration of feature extraction, fusion, and decision-making
within complex neural architectures. For example, attention mechanisms
learn relationships between representations, graph neural networks
aggregate information through relational structures, and generative
approaches can support cross-modal synthesis or reconstruction. This
classification is particularly relevant when two models operate at
similar fusion stages but employ substantially different computational
mechanisms. It also demonstrates why architectural comparisons should
consider the learning mechanism rather than relying exclusively on
the labels early fusion, intermediate fusion, or late fusion.

A complementary perspective is provided by Barua et al.
\citep{barua2023systematic}, who systematically reviewed multimodal
machine learning research across representation, translation, alignment,
fusion, and co-learning. Their review examined a large body of literature
to identify application trends, methodological challenges, and future
research opportunities. This broader perspective is important because
fusion is closely connected to the quality of the representations being
combined and the correspondence between modalities. A model may
incorporate an advanced fusion operator yet perform poorly when its
inputs are semantically inconsistent, temporally misaligned, or
insufficiently informative. Consequently, multimodal fusion should be
considered within the broader learning pipeline rather than as an
isolated architectural operation.

Domain-specific surveys provide additional insight into the suitability
of particular fusion strategies. Kalamkar and Geetha Mary
\citep{kalamkar2023imagefusion} presented a systematic review of
multimodal image fusion, covering traditional and deep learning-based
approaches, attention mechanisms, evaluation metrics, benchmark datasets,
and application areas such as medical imaging, remote sensing, and
surveillance. Their analysis provides detailed coverage of image-centric
fusion, in which the objective is often to combine complementary visual
information into a representation or image that preserves relevant
structural and contextual details. Nevertheless, image fusion represents
only one part of the broader multimodal learning problem. Integrating
images with text, audio, physiological measurements, or heterogeneous
sensor streams introduces additional requirements involving semantic
correspondence, temporal alignment, and modality-specific representation
learning. Accordingly, findings from image-fusion surveys should be
interpreted in relation to their particular tasks and evaluation settings.

Recent work has also examined multimodal fusion through the perspective
of self-supervised learning. Zong et al.
\citep{zong2025selfsupervised} surveyed self-supervised multimodal
learning, discussing representation learning without extensive manual
annotation, fusion architectures, and learning from unaligned data.
Their review highlights the importance of learning from large collections
of naturally occurring multimodal observations, where complete labels
or precisely aligned modality pairs may not be available. Self-supervised
objectives can encourage representations from related observations to
share useful information, while alignment strategies can help establish
correspondences across modalities. These approaches are relevant to
multimodal fusion because the availability of annotated, synchronized,
and balanced training data is often a limiting factor. However,
self-supervised learning describes a learning paradigm rather than
a particular fusion stage or mechanism, and should therefore be treated
as a separate dimension when classifying multimodal methods.

Several recent reviews have focused on biomedical and medical imaging
applications, where heterogeneous information can include images,
clinical measurements, physiological signals, and molecular data.
Duan et al. \citep{duan2024biomedicalfusion} reviewed deep learning-based
multimodal biomedical data fusion, examining data representations,
fusion strategies, available datasets, and challenges associated with
emerging generative and large-model approaches. Similarly, Li et al.
\citep{li2024medicalfusion} reviewed deep learning-based information
fusion for multimodal medical image classification. They discussed
input-level, intermediate, and output-level fusion, including
hierarchical and attention-based approaches, and examined the suitability
of different architectures for medical classification tasks. These
studies illustrate the value of application-specific analysis, particularly
when the interpretation, completeness, and reliability of fused
information have direct implications for downstream decisions. At the
same time, their medical focus limits the extent to which their findings
can be generalized directly to domains such as autonomous driving,
emotion recognition, robotics, and remote sensing.

Robustness has emerged as another important organizing principle in
recent multimodal surveys. Wu et al. \citep{wu2026missingmodality}
reviewed deep multimodal learning with missing modalities, examining
the causes of modality absence, approaches for learning from incomplete
inputs, relevant datasets, and open research challenges. Missingness
may arise from sensor failure, acquisition costs, privacy restrictions,
or incomplete data collection. Under these conditions, a fusion model
that assumes every modality is always available may experience
substantial performance degradation. This line of research motivates
the explicit evaluation of models under different modality-availability
patterns rather than relying exclusively on results obtained from
complete datasets.

Extending this data-centric perspective, Zhang et al.
\citep{zhang2026lowquality} surveyed multimodal fusion under low-quality
data conditions. Their taxonomy covers noisy, incomplete, imbalanced,
and dynamically varying modality quality. These conditions are related
but distinct: noise corrupts observed information, missingness removes
an information source, imbalance can cause unequal modality contributions,
and quality variation changes the reliability of individual modalities
across observations. Such distinctions are important because a method
that performs well when a modality is absent may not necessarily remain
effective when that modality is present but misleading or severely
degraded. The review therefore supports evaluating fusion approaches
under multiple realistic data conditions and considering reliability
alongside predictive performance.

More recently, Li and Tang \citep{li2026alignmentfusion} presented
a survey of multimodal alignment and fusion in the
\textit{International Journal of Computer Vision}. Their review
organizes fusion approaches from both structural and methodological
perspectives. Structural categories include data-level, feature-level,
and output-level integration, while methodological categories include
statistical, kernel-based, graph-based, generative, contrastive,
attention-based, and large language model-based approaches. The study
also discusses cross-modal misalignment, computational bottlenecks,
data quality, and the modality gap. This perspective is especially
relevant to contemporary fusion systems because alignment and fusion
are interdependent: the ability to combine information effectively
depends partly on whether representations from different sources
have learned compatible relationships. Although this survey includes
large language model-based approaches, the present review maintains
its primary focus on general deep learning fusion architectures,
their representation-learning strategies, and their behavior under
different data conditions, rather than attempting an exhaustive survey
of multimodal foundation models.

Taken together, these studies reveal that recent multimodal fusion
research is organized around several complementary questions:
where information is integrated, how cross-modal interactions are
modeled, how representations are learned, which application requirements
shape architectural choices, and how systems behave when input data
are incomplete or unreliable
\citep{jiao2024comprehensivesurvey,zhao2024deepfusion,
barua2023systematic,zong2025selfsupervised,
duan2024biomedicalfusion,li2024medicalfusion,
wu2026missingmodality,zhang2026lowquality,
li2026alignmentfusion}. No single perspective fully captures all
these dimensions. Stage-based classifications explain the location
of integration, technique-oriented taxonomies characterize the
underlying mechanisms, and domain- or condition-specific surveys
provide context for interpreting empirical results.

The MULTIFUSION-7 taxonomy is designed to complement these existing
contributions by characterizing each method along seven connected
dimensions: fusion stage, fusion mechanism, modality type, learning
paradigm, data condition, backbone architecture, and application domain.
Rather than claiming that earlier surveys omit these topics, the
framework brings the dimensions together to support more consistent
cross-study comparisons. For instance, two attention-based models may
differ in their fusion stages, backbone architectures, learning
objectives, or ability to handle missing and noisy modalities. Similarly,
models evaluated on different applications or under different data
conditions may not be directly comparable even when they use similar
fusion mechanisms.

Accordingly, the purpose of MULTIFUSION-7 is to provide a common
descriptive framework for examining the relationships between
architectural design, learning assumptions, data quality, and
application requirements. Its value depends on applying the
classification criteria consistently and grounding comparisons in
verifiable evidence from the reviewed studies. This cross-dimensional
perspective establishes the basis for the subsequent analysis of
fusion architectures, representation learning, robustness, and
evaluation practices.


\subsection{Representative Fusion Architectures and Mechanisms}

Method-specific papers help explain the assumptions that distinguish fusion mechanisms. ~\citep{zadeh2017tensor} introduced the Tensor Fusion Network for multimodal sentiment analysis, explicitly modeling intra-modal and inter-modal interactions through tensor-based fusion. ~\citep{tsai2019multimodal} proposed the Multimodal Transformer (MulT), using directional cross-modal attention to learn relationships between language, audio, and visual sequences without requiring explicit temporal alignment. These methods illustrate different design choices: tensor fusion provides an explicit representation of higher-order interactions, whereas cross-modal attention learns relationships between representations across modalities and time steps.

Such architectures should not be compared only by their names or their reported headline performance. Their behavior depends on factors such as the number and type of modalities, alignment assumptions, representation dimensions, objective functions, computational cost, and evaluation setting. In MULTIFUSION-7, we treat the fusion stage and mechanism as distinct dimensions: the stage describes where information is integrated, while the mechanism describes how we combine cross-modal information. The backbone architecture, learning paradigm, modality, and data conditions provide additional context for interpreting the method.


\subsection{Robustness and Missing-Modality Learning}
\label{subsec:robustness_missing}

The assumption that all modalities are consistently available and reliable is often violated in real-world applications. Sensor failures, privacy restrictions, acquisition costs, and incomplete data collection may result in missing modalities, while environmental interference can introduce noise and degrade input quality. Neverova et al.~\citep{neverova2016moddrop} introduced ModDrop, a training strategy that randomly drops modality-specific information to encourage robustness when some inputs are unavailable. More recent research has systematically examined missing-modality learning, highlighting strategies such as modality imputation, representation learning, and adaptive fusion \citep{wu2026missingmodality}. Robust fusion must also distinguish missing inputs from noisy or imbalanced modalities, as these conditions require different handling strategies \citep{zhang2026lowquality}. Consequently, multimodal systems should be evaluated under realistic data conditions rather than exclusively on complete and clean datasets.


~\citep{wu2026missingmodality} provide a recent survey specifically devoted to deep multimodal learning with missing modalities. Their review discusses the motivation for this setting, methods, applications, datasets, and open challenges. It offers a focused treatment of an important robustness problem, while the present review situates missing modalities alongside noisy inputs, modality imbalance, and temporal asynchrony. These conditions are related but distinct: a missing modality is unavailable, a noisy modality is present but corrupted, and modality imbalance can occur when one input contributes disproportionately to optimization or prediction. Explicitly treating these conditions matters when comparing results under different assumptions.

\subsection{Synthesis and Positioning of MULTIFUSION-7}

Table~\ref{tab:related-work-taxonomy} organizes representative literature into thematic groups rather than treating all cited works as equivalent surveys. Foundational surveys establish the main concepts and classifications; more recent reviews extend coverage of deep architectures and broad application settings; individual architecture papers demonstrate particular fusion mechanisms; and robustness-focused work studies incomplete data. These categories are complementary and indicate that the field already contains several useful taxonomies and survey-level syntheses.

MULTIFUSION-7 is designed as a cross-dimensional organization scheme for the review that follows. It connects (i) fusion stage, (ii) fusion mechanism, (iii) modality, (iv) learning paradigm, (v) data condition, (vi) backbone architecture, and (vii) application domain. The purpose is to make these dimensions visible together when describing a method, rather than to suggest that prior surveys ignored them entirely or that any single taxonomy resolves all methodological trade-offs. This framework's strength should ultimately be demonstrated through consistent literature screening, transparent classification criteria, and critical comparison of evidence across studies.

\section{Background and Preliminaries}
\label{sec:background}

This section introduces the fundamental concepts required to understand
multimodal fusion in deep learning. It first explains the nature of
multimodal data and the challenges associated with integrating heterogeneous
information sources. It then describes the principal deep learning
architectures used to extract modality-specific representations and
establishes the mathematical formulation of the fusion problem. These
preliminaries provide a common foundation for the taxonomy, architectural
comparisons, and robustness analysis presented in the subsequent sections.
For broader discussions of multimodal machine learning and deep multimodal
representation learning, we refer readers to
\citep{atrey2010multimodal,ramachandram2017deep,baltrusaitis2019survey}.

\subsection{Fundamentals of Multimodal Data}
\label{subsec:multimodal_fundamentals}

\subsubsection{Definition of Modality and Multimodal Learning}

A modality refers to a particular type of information or a distinct
channel through which an observation of the world is obtained. Examples
include images, natural language, speech, video, physiological measurements,
and signals collected from environmental sensors. Each modality captures
different characteristics of an underlying object, event, or phenomenon.
For example, a traffic scene may be represented by camera images, LiDAR
point clouds, radar measurements, and textual descriptions. Although these
sources may describe the same scene, they differ in their structures,
statistical properties, and information content.

A learning system that processes information from two or more such sources
is commonly described as a multimodal learning system. In contrast to
unimodal learning, which relies on a single source of information,
multimodal learning attempts to exploit relationships between multiple
sources to obtain a more informative representation or a more reliable
prediction. The general objective is not simply to combine a larger volume
of data, but to learn how different sources contribute complementary
evidence to a particular task \citep{ramachandram2017deep,baltrusaitis2019survey}.

Let $M$ denote the number of modalities available to a learning system.
For an observation indexed by $i$, the multimodal input can be represented
as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_i =
    \left(
    \mathbf{x}_i^{(1)},
    \mathbf{x}_i^{(2)},
    \ldots,
    \mathbf{x}_i^{(M)}
    \right),
    \label{eq:multimodal_input}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}_i^{(m)}$ denotes the observation obtained from modality
$m$. The target associated with this observation is denoted by $y_i$,
which may represent a class label, a continuous value, a sequence, or
another task-specific output.

For example, in multimodal emotion recognition, an observation may contain
a spoken utterance, its corresponding audio signal, and a video showing
the speaker's facial expressions. The target may be a sentiment category
or a continuous sentiment score. Each modality provides a different
perspective on the same utterance, and the learning system attempts to
integrate these perspectives to improve prediction.

depending on the application. Table~\ref{tab:modalities} summarizes the
principal modality types considered in this review.

These modalities differ not only in their semantic content but also in
their mathematical representations. An image is typically represented
as a multidimensional array of pixel values, text as a sequence of tokens,
audio as a waveform or a time--frequency representation, and a sensor
stream as a sequence of numerical measurements. Consequently, a single
model architecture cannot always process every modality effectively
without an appropriate representation and encoding strategy.

The choice of modality also shapes a fusion system requirements.
For instance, image-text fusion may require semantic correspondence
between visual objects and linguistic descriptions, whereas audio--visual
fusion may require temporal correspondence between speech and facial
movements. Sensor fusion in autonomous driving may additionally require
spatial calibration and geometric correspondence between measurements
collected by different devices.

\subsubsection{Heterogeneity, Complementarity, and Redundancy}

Three fundamental properties help explain why multimodal fusion is
challenging and potentially beneficial: i) heterogeneity, ii) complementarity,
and iii) redundancy.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Heterogeneity refers to the differences between modalities in
their data structures, dimensionality, distributions, sampling rates,
and noise characteristics. For example, a text encoder may produce a
sequence of contextual token embeddings, whereas a visual encoder may
produce a spatial feature map or a sequence of image patches. These
representations cannot necessarily be combined directly because their
dimensions and semantic organization differ.

 \item Complementarity occurs when different modalities provide distinct
but mutually useful information. In medical diagnosis, an image may reveal
anatomical abnormalities, while a clinical note may describe symptoms,
medical history, and other contextual evidence. A model that integrates
both sources may obtain a more complete representation than a model that
uses either source independently, provided that the information is
relevant and the integration is learned appropriately.

 \item Redundancy occurs when multiple modalities encode overlapping
information. For example, a camera and a LiDAR sensor may both provide
evidence about the location of an object, although their measurements
represent that evidence differently. Redundant information can improve
reliability when sources agree, but it can also increase computational
cost or amplify shared errors if it is not handled carefully.
\end{enumerate}
These properties are not mutually exclusive. A pair of modalities may
contain complementary information about some aspects of an observation
and redundant information about others. Effective fusion therefore
requires a model to preserve useful modality-specific information while
learning meaningful relationships across modalities

\citep{baltrusaitis2019survey}.

\subsubsection{Temporal and Semantic Alignment}

Before information from different modalities can be integrated
effectively, the model must establish an appropriate relationship between
their observations. This relationship is commonly discussed in terms of
alignment.
\begin{enumerate}
 
   \item Temporal alignment concerns the correspondence between
observations acquired at different time steps. In a spoken interaction,
for example, the audio signal may be sampled at a much higher frequency
than the video frames. A word, its acoustic realization, and the
corresponding facial movement may therefore be represented by sequences
of different lengths.

   \item Spatial alignment concerns correspondence between measurements
in a common geometric reference frame. In autonomous driving, camera
pixels and LiDAR points must be related appropriately if the model is
to associate visual appearance with three-dimensional object geometry.

   \item Semantic alignment concerns whether representations from
different modalities refer to related concepts or events. For example,
an image embedding and a text embedding may need to represent the same
object or scene in a compatible semantic space, even when the underlying
data have very different structures.
\end{enumerate}
Alignment does not always require one-to-one correspondence between
individual elements. Some architectures, including the Multimodal
Transformer, learn cross-modal relationships between sequences without
requiring explicit temporal alignment
\citep{tsai2019multimodal}. Nevertheless, the suitability of a particular
alignment strategy depends on the task, the data acquisition process,
and the architecture.

\subsubsection{Data Availability and Reliability}

Real-world multimodal data are not always complete or equally reliable.
A sensor may fail, an audio recording may contain background noise, an
image may be blurred, or a modality may be unavailable because of
privacy or acquisition constraints. A fusion model must therefore be
understood in relation to the conditions under which its input data
are collected and processed.

Let $\mathcal{A}_i$ denote the set of modalities available for observation
$i$, where
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{A}_i \subseteq \{1,2,\ldots,M\}.
    \label{eq:available_modalities}
\end{equation}
When every modality is available, $\mathcal{A}_i$ contains all $M$
modalities. When one or more modalities are absent, the available set
contains only the modalities observed for that sample.

Several data conditions should be distinguished. \textit{Missingness}
means that a modality or part of a modality is unavailable.
\textit{Noise} means that a modality is present but its measurements
are corrupted or degraded. \textit{Modality imbalance} occurs when
the modalities contribute unequally to learning or prediction, potentially
causing the model to rely excessively on one source. \textit{Asynchrony}
occurs when observations from different modalities are not temporally
aligned.

These conditions require different responses. Missing information may
require modality-invariant representations, modality dropout, or
imputation; noisy information may require robust feature extraction
or uncertainty-aware weighting; and asynchronous observations may
require sequence alignment or cross-modal attention. ModDrop, for
example, randomly removes modality-specific information during
training to encourage models to remain useful when some modalities
are unavailable \citep{neverova2016moddrop}. The distinction between
these conditions is important because performance on complete,
clean datasets does not by itself establish robustness under
realistic deployment conditions.


\subsection{Deep Learning Backbones for Multimodal Fusion}
\label{subsec:deep_learning_backbones}

Deep learning backbones provide the representational foundation of
multimodal fusion systems by transforming heterogeneous inputs into
features that can be processed by a fusion module or a task-specific
prediction head. The choice of backbone determines which patterns
can be extracted efficiently, how temporal or spatial dependencies
are represented, and how readily information from different modalities
can be integrated. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent
neural networks (RNNs), graph neural networks (GNNs), Transformers,
autoencoders, generative adversarial networks (GANs), and diffusion
models offer complementary capabilities rather than interchangeable
solutions \citep{ramachandram2017deep,baltrusaitis2019survey,
xu2023multimodaltransformers}.

Recent developments further demonstrate that the effectiveness of a
backbone depends on the relationships between its architecture,
training objective, input modalities, and fusion strategy. ImageBind,
for example, learns a shared embedding space across images, text,
audio, depth, thermal imagery, and inertial measurements, illustrating
how modality-specific representations can be connected through a
common embedding framework \citep{girdhar2023imagebind}. More recent
work has also investigated the relationship between native multimodal
architectures, fusion strategies, and model scaling
\citep{shukor2025scaling}. Accordingly, this subsection examines
the principal backbone families, their mathematical foundations,
their contributions to multimodal fusion, and their limitations.

\subsubsection{Role of a Backbone Architecture}

A backbone is the neural network responsible for extracting
task-relevant information from an input modality. In a multimodal
system, separate encoders are often employed because images, text,
audio, video, and sensor measurements have different structures
and statistical properties. For example, a visual encoder may
extract spatial features from an image, while a sequential encoder
models the temporal dependencies in an audio signal.

Let $M$ denote the number of modalities and let
$\mathbf{x}_i^{(m)}$ represent modality $m$ for observation $i$.
The corresponding modality-specific representation is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}_i^{(m)}
    =
    \phi_m\left(
    \mathbf{x}_i^{(m)};\boldsymbol{\theta}_m
    \right),
    \label{eq:modality_encoder}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_m$ denotes the encoder and
$\boldsymbol{\theta}_m$ represents its learnable parameters.
Depending on the architecture, $\mathbf{h}_i^{(m)}$ may be a feature
vector, a sequence of embeddings, a spatial feature map, or a set
of graph-node representations.

When the resulting features have incompatible dimensions or
representation formats, a projection function can be applied:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_i^{(m)}
    =
    p_m\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{(m)}\right),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{z}_i^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_m'},
    \label{eq:modality_projection}
\end{equation}
where $p_m$ is a learnable or predefined transformation and
$d_m'$ denotes the projected feature dimensionality. A common
embedding dimension is useful for certain attention-based or
similarity-based methods, but it is not necessary for every fusion
architecture.

The backbone and the fusion module have distinct conceptual roles.
The backbone extracts information within a modality, whereas the
fusion module models relationships between modalities or aggregates
their representations. These components may be optimized jointly
or separately. Joint optimization allows the task objective to
influence feature extraction, whereas separately pretrained encoders
can reduce training requirements and exploit representations learned
from larger datasets.

This distinction also clarifies an important architectural issue:
the same backbone can participate in different fusion strategies,
and a single fusion strategy can incorporate different backbones.
Consequently, backbone architecture should be analyzed independently
from fusion stage, fusion mechanism, and learning paradigm
\citep{ramachandram2017deep,xu2023multimodaltransformers}.

\subsubsection{Convolutional Neural Networks}

Convolutional neural networks are widely used to extract local and
hierarchical features from spatially structured data. Their
convolutional filters detect local patterns, while successive layers
combine these patterns into increasingly abstract representations.
This inductive bias makes CNNs particularly effective for images,
video frames, depth maps, spectrograms, and spatially organized
sensor measurements \citep{lecun1998gradient}.

In multimodal fusion, a CNN may encode a camera image while another
encoder processes textual descriptions, LiDAR measurements, or
physiological signals. The resulting representations can then be
combined through concatenation, attention, gating, or another
fusion mechanism. CNN-based feature extraction is especially
appropriate when local spatial structure and hierarchical visual
patterns are important to the prediction task.

Modern convolutional architectures also demonstrate that CNNs remain
competitive with attention-based visual encoders. ConvNeXt, for
example, revisits conventional convolutional design using architectural
choices informed by the development of Vision Transformers
\citep{liu2022convnext}. Such architectures can serve as effective
visual backbones in multimodal systems without requiring every
modality to be processed by a Transformer.

Nevertheless, conventional CNNs do not automatically establish
semantic correspondence between heterogeneous modalities. Their
features must be connected through an appropriate fusion operation
or learning objective. Moreover, standard convolutional operations
primarily encode local spatial structure; long-range relationships
or temporal dependencies may require additional architectural
components. CNNs should therefore be regarded as modality encoders
whose effectiveness depends on the downstream fusion design.

\subsubsection{Recurrent Neural Networks and Long Short-Term Memory}

Recurrent neural networks are designed for sequential observations
in which the order of elements carries meaningful information.
They maintain a hidden state that summarizes information from
previous time steps. Given an input sequence
$\mathbf{x}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_T$, a recurrent update can be
expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}_t
    =
    f_{\theta}\left(
    \mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{h}_{t-1}
    \right),
    \label{eq:rnn_update}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}_t$ is the hidden representation at time $t$,
$\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ is the preceding hidden state, and
$f_{\theta}$ is a learnable recurrent transformation.

Long short-term memory networks extend this formulation with a
memory cell and gating operations that regulate information
retention, updating, and output. These mechanisms were introduced
to improve the learning of dependencies over extended sequences
\citep{hochreiter1997long}. In multimodal systems, RNNs and LSTMs
can encode speech, audio, textual sequences, physiological
measurements, and time-dependent sensor streams before their
representations are integrated with other modalities.

For example, an LSTM may summarize the temporal dynamics of speech,
while a CNN extracts facial features from corresponding video frames.
A fusion module can subsequently combine these representations
for emotion recognition. Such a design separates temporal
feature extraction from cross-modal integration, although it still
requires an appropriate strategy for handling different sampling
rates and temporal alignment.

The principal limitation of recurrent architectures is their
sequential computation, which can restrict parallel processing.
Furthermore, recurrent memory does not itself solve cross-modal
alignment: audio and visual sequences may still require
synchronization or a learned cross-modal interaction mechanism.
Transformers provide an alternative when long-range relationships
and parallel sequence processing are important
\citep{xu2023multimodaltransformers}.

\subsubsection{Graph Neural Networks}

Graph neural networks learn representations from relational data
represented by nodes and edges. Nodes may correspond to objects,
entities, sensor observations, image regions, or modality-specific
features, while edges encode relationships such as spatial proximity,
temporal correspondence, semantic association, or physical interaction.
This representation is particularly useful when the data contain
irregular relationships that cannot be described adequately by a
regular grid or a fixed-length sequence.

A generic message-passing operation updates the representation
of node $v$ at layer $l$ as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}_v^{(l+1)}
    =
    \operatorname{UPDATE}^{(l)}
    \left(
    \mathbf{h}_v^{(l)},
    \operatorname{AGGREGATE}^{(l)}
    \left(
    \left\{
    \mathbf{h}_u^{(l)}:
    u\in\mathcal{N}(v)
    \right\}
    \right)
    \right),
    \label{eq:gnn_message_passing}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}(v)$ denotes the neighborhood of node $v$.
The aggregation function combines information from neighboring
nodes, and the update function uses this information to produce
the next representation. Graph convolutional networks provide
one established formulation of this process
\citep{kipf2017semi}.

In multimodal fusion, a graph can connect heterogeneous observations
and propagate information across their relationships. For example,
an autonomous-driving system may represent detected objects as
nodes and encode their spatial relationships as edges. Similarly,
multimodal biomedical systems can use graph structures to represent
relationships among clinical variables, imaging features, or
biological entities. Recent survey work on multimodal GNNs in
Earth observation also emphasizes their potential for integrating
remote-sensing imagery, geospatial structures, and environmental
sensor measurements \citep{kaur2026multimodalgnn}.

The effectiveness of graph-based fusion depends on the quality
of the graph structure, the relevance of the edges, and the
aggregation mechanism. Incorrect edges can propagate misleading
information, while constructing and processing large heterogeneous
graphs can introduce additional computational costs. GNNs are
therefore particularly suitable when relational structure is
important, rather than a universal replacement for convolutional
or sequence-based encoders.

\subsubsection{Transformers and Attention Mechanisms}

Transformers use attention to model dependencies between elements
of a sequence or a set of representations. Their attention mechanism
allows each element to aggregate information from other elements
according to learned relevance scores. This capability is useful
for modeling long-range dependencies and interactions between
different modalities \citep{vaswani2017attention}.

Scaled dot-product attention is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})
    =
    \operatorname{softmax}
    \left(
    \frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}
    \right)\mathbf{V},
    \label{eq:scaled_dot_product_attention}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are the query,
key, and value matrices, respectively, and $d_k$ is the key
dimensionality. The query--key similarities determine the attention
weights, which are then used to aggregate the value vectors.

In self-attention, the queries, keys, and values are derived from
the same modality or sequence. In cross-attention, queries may be
derived from one modality, while keys and values are obtained from
another. For instance, textual features can attend to visual
features to identify image regions relevant to a sentence.
The Multimodal Transformer (MulT) demonstrates how directional
cross-modal attention can model language, audio, and visual
sequences without requiring explicit temporal alignment
\citep{tsai2019multimodal}.

Recent research has extended Transformer-based multimodal learning
from task-specific fusion to shared embedding spaces and broader
cross-modal representation learning. The survey by Xu et al.
systematically reviews multimodal Transformer architectures,
pretraining strategies, applications, and unresolved challenges
\citep{xu2023multimodaltransformers}. ImageBind further demonstrates
how image-paired training data can support a common embedding
space across several modalities, including text, audio, depth,
thermal imagery, and inertial measurements
\citep{girdhar2023imagebind}.

Transformer-based fusion is flexible, but its effectiveness depends
on the number of tokens, the quality of the modality representations,
and the training data. Standard self-attention has quadratic
complexity in sequence length, which can become expensive for
long video streams or dense sensor sequences. Moreover, attention
weights alone do not guarantee interpretability or robustness.
Efficient attention, modality-specific encoders, and appropriate
training objectives may therefore be necessary for practical
multimodal deployment.

Recent scaling studies also caution against assuming that one
fusion architecture is universally superior. Shukor et al. compare
native multimodal models across different architectural configurations
and training scales, showing that the relative performance of early-
and late-fusion designs depends on the experimental setting
\citep{shukor2025scaling}. This reinforces the need to evaluate
architectures under comparable data, model-size, and computational
conditions.

\subsubsection{Autoencoders and Variational Autoencoders}

Autoencoders learn compact representations by encoding an input
and reconstructing it from a latent representation. For modality
$m$, the encoder--decoder process can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}^{(m)}
    =
    E_m\left(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}\right),
    \qquad
    \widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(m)}
    =
    D_m\left(\mathbf{z}^{(m)}\right),
    \label{eq:autoencoder}
\end{equation}
where $E_m$ and $D_m$ denote the encoder and decoder, respectively,
and $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(m)}$ is the reconstructed input.
A conventional reconstruction objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
    =
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \lambda_m
    \mathbb{E}
    \left[
    d_m\left(
    \mathbf{x}^{(m)},
    \widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(m)}
    \right)
    \right],
    \label{eq:reconstruction_loss}
\end{equation}
where $d_m$ measures reconstruction error and $\lambda_m$
controls the contribution of modality $m$.

In multimodal systems, autoencoders can learn modality-specific
representations, shared latent spaces, or a combination of shared
and private features. Shared representations aim to capture
information common to multiple modalities, whereas private
representations preserve modality-specific details. This distinction
is important when the modalities are related but not interchangeable.

Variational autoencoders (VAEs) extend the autoencoder framework
by learning probability distributions over latent variables rather
than only deterministic codes. This probabilistic formulation
supports representation learning, conditional generation, and
reasoning about incomplete observations. Recent work by Qiu et al.
develops a barycentric formulation of multimodal VAEs, providing
a principled way to combine modality-specific inference distributions
while accounting for both modality-invariant and modality-specific
information \citep{qiu2025multimodalvae}.

Autoencoder-based models can be valuable when labeled data are
limited or reconstruction is an important training objective.
They may also support missing-modality learning by estimating
representations of unavailable information. However, reconstruction
quality does not guarantee task-relevant features, and a generated
or reconstructed modality should not be treated as an exact recovery
of the original observation.

\subsubsection{Generative Adversarial Networks}

Generative adversarial networks consist of a generator and a
discriminator trained through a competitive objective
\citep{goodfellow2014generative}. The generator learns to produce
realistic samples, while the discriminator learns to distinguish
generated samples from real observations. In multimodal settings,
conditional generators can translate information from one modality
into another or synthesize a candidate representation when an
input modality is unavailable.

For example, a generator may produce a visual representation
conditioned on text or estimate a missing image from another
imaging modality. The generated representation can subsequently
be integrated with observed features by a fusion module. This
approach can be useful when modalities have complementary
information but are not all available at inference time.

Nevertheless, adversarial training can be difficult to stabilize,
and generated samples may omit important details or introduce
plausible but incorrect information. In applications where
prediction reliability is critical, generative quality should
therefore be evaluated alongside downstream task performance
and robustness to uncertainty.

\subsubsection{Diffusion Models}

Diffusion models learn to generate data by reversing a gradual
corruption process. In a typical formulation, noise is added to
training samples over multiple steps, and a neural network learns
to estimate or remove the introduced noise. Generation proceeds
through iterative denoising, optionally conditioned on other
observed modalities \citep{ho2020denoising}.

For multimodal fusion, diffusion models can support conditional
generation, representation refinement, and estimation of missing
modalities. Their role is particularly relevant when the information
provided by one modality can guide the reconstruction of another.
However, the generated output remains an estimate and may not
faithfully reproduce the missing observation.

Recent conference studies illustrate the use of diffusion within
multimodal learning rather than only as a standalone image generator.
Dai et al. introduce a multi-stage diffusion architecture for
missing-modality recovery and address the imbalance that can arise
when different modalities are reconstructed with different fidelity
\citep{dai2025unbiased}. At the application level, DiffuFuse uses
diffusion-based semantic modeling within a dual-stream framework
for multimodal sentiment analysis involving text, audio, and visual
information \citep{lv2025diffufuse}.

These studies illustrate the potential of diffusion-based approaches
for modeling complex cross-modal dependencies. Their principal
trade-offs include iterative inference cost, training complexity,
and uncertainty in generated information. Consequently, diffusion
models should be assessed not only by the visual or semantic quality
of their outputs, but also by their effect on downstream prediction,
reliability, and computational efficiency.

\subsection{Fundamentals of Multimodal Fusion}
\label{subsec:fusion_fundamentals}

\subsubsection{Definition and General Mathematical Formulation}

Multimodal fusion is the process of integrating information from
multiple modalities to produce a representation or prediction that
is useful for a particular task. Fusion can combine raw inputs,
learned features, intermediate hidden states, model outputs, or
information exchanged across different processing stages.

Let $\mathbf{z}^{(m)}$ denote the representation obtained from
modality $m$. A general fusion function can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{fused}}
    =
    F\left(
    \mathbf{z}^{(1)},
    \mathbf{z}^{(2)},
    \ldots,
    \mathbf{z}^{(M)}
    \right),
    \label{eq:general_fusion}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is the fusion operator and
$\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{fused}}$ is the resulting joint representation.

The fused representation is subsequently passed to a task-specific
prediction function $g$:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{y}
    =
    g\left(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{fused}}\right).
    \label{eq:fusion_prediction}
\end{equation}
For classification, $g$ may produce class probabilities; for regression,
it may produce a continuous estimate; and for sequence prediction,
it may generate an output sequence.

Combining these expressions gives the general multimodal prediction
model:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{y}
    =
    g\left(
    F\left(
    \phi_1(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}),
    \ldots,
    \phi_M(\mathbf{x}^{(M)})
    \right)
    \right),
    \label{eq:complete_fusion_model}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_m$ is the encoder for modality $m$. This formulation
separates three conceptual operations: encoding the individual
modalities, integrating their representations, and producing the
task-specific output. In an end-to-end trainable system, these
components can be optimized jointly.

\subsubsection{Learning Objective}

The principal objective of a supervised multimodal model is to
minimize prediction error on training examples. Given a dataset
$\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, a generic empirical
training objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \ell\left(
    \widehat{y}_i,y_i
    \right),
    \label{eq:task_loss}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of training examples and $\ell$ is a
task-specific loss function. Examples include cross-entropy for
classification and mean squared error for regression.

The training objective may include additional terms that encourage
useful properties, such as agreement between related modalities,
robustness to missing inputs, or reconstruction of modality-specific
information. A general objective can therefore be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}
    +
    \sum_{r=1}^{R}
    \lambda_r\mathcal{L}_r,
    \label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_r$ denotes an auxiliary objective and $\lambda_r$
controls its contribution. Not every multimodal model requires
auxiliary objectives; their use depends on the architecture and
learning paradigm.

\subsubsection{Fusion Stage: Where Information Is Integrated}

One of the most fundamental distinctions between fusion strategies
concerns the stage at which information from different modalities
is combined. The fusion stage describes \textit{where} integration
occurs in the processing pipeline, rather than the particular
mathematical mechanism used to perform it
\citep{atrey2010multimodal,ramachandram2017deep}.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Early fusion combines inputs or low-level features before
substantial modality-specific processing has occurred. It can expose
cross-modal relationships early, but it may require compatible
representations and appropriate alignment.

 \item Intermediate fusion combines representations within hidden
layers of the network. Each modality can first be processed by a
suitable encoder, after which the learned features interact through
a fusion module. This arrangement allows modality-specific processing
and cross-modal learning to coexist.

 \item Late fusion combines predictions, class probabilities, or
other high-level outputs from separate modality-specific models.
Because each model can process its own input independently, late
fusion can be useful when the modalities differ substantially in
structure or availability. However, it may lose fine-grained
cross-modal relationships that could have been learned by integrating
features earlier.

 \item Hybrid fusion combines more than one fusion stage within
the same architecture. For example, a model may integrate visual
and spatial features within intermediate layers and subsequently
combine the resulting prediction with a separate textual model.

 \item Adaptive fusion adjusts the contribution of different
modalities according to the input, learned reliability estimates,
or contextual information. Unlike a fixed combination rule,
adaptive fusion can assign different weights to different modalities
for different observations.

These categories describe related but distinct architectural choices.
A hybrid system may also be adaptive, and an attention-based mechanism
may be used at either intermediate or later stages. Therefore, fusion
stage alone is insufficient to characterize the complete design of
a multimodal model.
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection{Fusion Mechanism: How Information Is Integrated}

In addition to the fusion stage, multimodal models differ in the
mechanism used to combine representations. The mechanism determines
how information is aggregated, how interactions between modalities
are modeled, and which assumptions the architecture makes about
their relationships.
\begin{enumerate}
   
 \item Concatenation-based fusion joins feature vectors into a
single representation:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{fused}}
    =
    \left[
    \mathbf{z}^{(1)};
    \mathbf{z}^{(2)};
    \ldots;
    \mathbf{z}^{(M)}
    \right],
    \label{eq:concatenation_fusion}
\end{equation}
where the semicolon denotes concatenation along the feature dimension.
A subsequent neural network can learn how to combine the resulting
features. Concatenation is simple and preserves the individual
representations, but it does not explicitly model higher-order
interactions by itself.

 \item Attention-based fusion learns relationships between elements
or features and uses the resulting weights to aggregate relevant
information. Self-attention models relationships within a modality,
whereas cross-attention allows one modality to select information
from another. These mechanisms are particularly useful when different
parts of a sequence or different regions of an image contribute
unequally to a prediction.

 \item Tensor-based fusion explicitly represents interactions
between features from multiple modalities. Tensor Fusion Networks,
for example, model both intra-modal and inter-modal interactions
for multi-modal sentiment analysis \citep{zadeh2017tensor}. Such
representations can capture complex relationships, but their
dimensionality and computational requirements can increase rapidly
as the number of modalities or feature dimensions grows.

 \item Graph-based fusion represents observations or entities as
nodes connected by relationships. Information is then integrated
through message passing or other graph operations. This is useful
when relational structure is important to the task.

\item Probabilistic fusion represents uncertainty in modality-specific
measurements or predictions and combines evidence under an appropriate probabilistic formulation. Its usefulness depends on the validity of the uncertainty estimates and the assumptions of the combination model.

\item Contrastive approaches learn representations by encouraging
related cross-modal examples to be similar and unrelated examples
to be distinguishable in a representation space. Such objectives
can improve cross-modal retrieval and representation alignment.
However, contrastive learning is primarily a learning objective or
representation-learning strategy; it does not, by itself, specify
the location of fusion in a network.

\item Gated fusion uses learnable gates or weights to regulate
how much information each modality contributes. It can be useful
when the relevance or reliability of a modality varies between
observations.

\item Generative fusion uses generative models to synthesize,
reconstruct, or estimate modality-specific information. It can help
when inputs are incomplete, although the generated information may
not exactly match the unavailable observation.
\end{enumerate}
These mechanisms can be combined within one system. For example,
a model may use cross-attention to establish relationships between
text and visual features, gating to regulate their relative influence,
and a task-specific prediction head to generate the final output.

\subsubsection{Information Complementarity and Modality Contribution}

A central motivation for multimodal fusion is that the joint input
may contain information that is unavailable from an individual
modality. Nevertheless, adding modalities does not automatically
improve predictive performance. An additional modality may be
irrelevant, noisy, misaligned, or difficult to learn from, and its
inclusion can increase the risk of overfitting or optimization
imbalance.

For this reason, a fusion system should be evaluated against
appropriate unimodal baselines. Let
$\widehat{y}^{(m)}$ denote the prediction obtained using only modality
$m$, and let $\widehat{y}_{\mathrm{fused}}$ denote the prediction
from the multimodal model. Comparing their performance helps
determine whether the integration provides a meaningful benefit.

Such comparisons should use the same evaluation data and, where
appropriate, comparable training conditions. They should also
distinguish improvements in average predictive performance from
improvements in robustness when some modalities are missing or
degraded.

The relative contribution of each modality may also vary across
observations. In an adaptive model, the fused representation can
be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{fused}}
    =
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \alpha_m(\mathbf{x})
    \,\psi_m\left(\mathbf{z}^{(m)}\right),
    \label{eq:weighted_fusion}
\end{equation}
where $\psi_m$ is a transformation of modality $m$ and
$\alpha_m(\mathbf{x})$ is an input-dependent contribution weight.
When these weights are constrained to be nonnegative and sum to one,
they can be interpreted as relative mixture weights. Other fusion
architectures may use unconstrained gates, multiplicative interactions,
or more complex aggregation functions.

Although learned weights can help explain how a model combines
modalities, they should not automatically be interpreted as definitive
measures of causal importance or model reliability. Their meaning
depends on the architecture, the learned representations, and the
behavior of the complete prediction system.

\subsubsection{A Unified View of the Fusion Pipeline}

The preceding discussion establishes a common framework for
describing multimodal fusion systems. Each modality is first
represented by an appropriate encoder, producing features that
preserve task-relevant information. A fusion mechanism then combines
these features at a particular stage of the processing pipeline.
Finally, a prediction head maps the fused representation to the
desired output.

The complete design depends on several interacting decisions:
the modalities being integrated, the backbone architectures used
to encode them, the stage and mechanism of fusion, the learning
objective, and the conditions under which the data are available.
These choices determine not only predictive performance but also
computational cost, robustness, interpretability, and generalization
to unseen data.

The distinctions introduced in this section provide the conceptual
basis for the MULTIFUSION-7 taxonomy presented in the following
section. In particular, distinguishing fusion stage from fusion
mechanism prevents different architectural choices from being treated
as interchangeable. Likewise, distinguishing backbone architecture
from learning paradigm and data condition makes it possible to
describe a method more completely than by its model name alone.
This unified perspective supports the systematic comparison of
multimodal fusion approaches across architectures, applications,
and practical operating conditions.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methology}

\subsection{MULTIFUSION-7 Taxonomy}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

Multimodal fusion encompasses a broad range of architectures, learning
strategies, data conditions, and application requirements. Consequently,
classifying methods solely according to where fusion occurs in a
processing pipeline provides an incomplete description of contemporary
deep learning systems. Earlier surveys established distinctions between
feature-level, decision-level, and hybrid fusion
\citep{atrey2010multimodal,ramachandram2017deep}, while subsequent
reviews have expanded the classification of methods to include
attention mechanisms, graph neural networks, generative architectures,
and constraint-based approaches
\citep{jiao2024comprehensivesurvey,zhao2024deepfusion}.
Recent work further emphasizes the relationships between alignment,
fusion mechanisms, and data quality
\citep{li2026alignmentfusion,zhang2026lowquality}.

To provide a common descriptive framework for comparing these
complementary perspectives, we introduce MULTIFUSION-7, a
seven-dimensional taxonomy for organizing multimodal fusion research.
The framework characterizes each method according to
(i) fusion stage, (ii) fusion mechanism, (iii) modality type,
(iv) learning paradigm, (v) data condition, (vi) backbone architecture,
and (vii) application domain. These dimensions capture different
aspects of a multimodal system and are intended to be considered
together rather than as isolated lists of architectures or techniques.

Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy} illustrates the overall structure of
MULTIFUSION-7 and the principal categories associated with each
dimension. The framework integrates conventional fusion-stage
classifications with mechanism-oriented descriptions, while explicitly
accounting for learning objectives, input availability, architectural
choices, and application requirements. This broader view is important
because two methods using the same fusion mechanism may differ in
their backbone architectures, assumptions about modality availability,
or suitability for a particular task.

Formally, a multimodal fusion method $m$ can be described by the
classification tuple
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}(m)
    =
    \left(
    S_m,\,
    F_m,\,
    \mathcal{M}_m,\,
    L_m,\,
    D_m,\,
    B_m,\,
    A_m
    \right),
    \label{eq:multifusion7_tuple}
\end{equation}
where $S_m$ denotes the fusion stage, $F_m$ the fusion mechanism,
$\mathcal{M}_m$ the set of modalities, $L_m$ the learning paradigm,
$D_m$ the data condition, $B_m$ the backbone architecture, and
$A_m$ the application domain. A method may receive multiple labels
within a dimension when it combines several mechanisms, uses more
than one modality, or is evaluated under multiple data conditions.

MULTIFUSION-7 is therefore a descriptive and comparative taxonomy,
not a new fusion algorithm or a claim that the seven dimensions
are statistically independent. Its purpose is to make the design
choices and assumptions of individual studies explicit, thereby
supporting more consistent comparisons across methods and domains.

learning. The framework organizes the literature along seven

\end{figure}


\subsection{Dimension 1: Fusion Stage}
\label{subsec:fusion_stage}

Fusion stage describes where information from different modalities
is integrated within a processing pipeline. Conventional classifications
distinguish early, intermediate, and late fusion, while hybrid
architectures combine multiple stages
\citep{atrey2010multimodal,jiao2024comprehensivesurvey,
li2026alignmentfusion}. MULTIFUSION-7 additionally records adaptive
fusion to represent systems that dynamically regulate the contribution
of individual modalities.
\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Early fusion:}
    Information is combined at the input level or through
    low-level features before substantial modality-specific
    processing. For example, aligned sensor measurements may
    be concatenated before being passed to a shared encoder.
    Early fusion can expose cross-modal interactions at an
    early stage, but it may require compatible representations
    and appropriate alignment.

    \item \textbf{Intermediate fusion:}
    Modality-specific encoders first extract features, which are
    subsequently integrated within one or more hidden layers.
    Cross-attention, co-attention, feature concatenation, and
    tensor-based interactions can be used at this stage.
    Intermediate fusion allows modality-specific processing
    while supporting learned cross-modal relationships.

    \item \textbf{Late fusion:}
    Separate modality-specific models produce predictions,
    class probabilities, or other high-level representations
    that are combined by a decision-level aggregation function.
    This strategy supports modular architectures and can
    accommodate heterogeneous input pipelines, although it
    may discard fine-grained interactions that could have
    been learned from intermediate features.

    \item \textbf{Hybrid fusion:}
    Multiple fusion stages are incorporated within a single
    architecture. A model, for example, may integrate visual
    and spatial features at an intermediate layer and then
    combine the resulting prediction with an independently
    generated textual prediction. Hybrid designs can capture
    complementary information at different levels but may
    increase architectural and optimization complexity.

    \item \textbf{Adaptive fusion:}
    The contribution of each modality is adjusted according
    to the input, context, or estimated reliability. Learned
    gates, attention weights, and input-dependent mixture
    coefficients can implement this behavior
    \citep{ovalle2017gated,shen2025adaptivefusion}.
    Adaptive fusion is not strictly a separate temporal stage:
    it can operate during early, intermediate, or late fusion.
    In MULTIFUSION-7, it is recorded as an additional
    architectural property whenever modality contributions
    are dynamically determined.

\end{enumerate}

These categories are not mutually exclusive. A model may employ
intermediate cross-attention together with adaptive gating, or
combine intermediate and late fusion in a hybrid architecture.
Accordingly, classification should identify the primary integration
stage and record any additional stages or adaptive operations.
This distinction prevents the location of fusion from being
confused with the mechanism used to perform it
\citep{zhao2024deepfusion,li2026alignmentfusion}.


\subsection{Dimension 2: Fusion Mechanism}
\label{subsec:fusion_mechanism}

than where integration occurs. Contemporary deep learning methods
employ a range of operations to combine modality-specific features,
model relationships between observations, or learn shared
representations. Zhao et al.~\citep{zhao2024deepfusion} organize
deep multimodal fusion methods into technique-oriented families,
while Li and Tang~\citep{li2026alignmentfusion} examine statistical,
kernel-based, graph-based, generative, contrastive, and
attention-based approaches. Building on these perspectives,
MULTIFUSION-7 records eight broad mechanism categories.

    layers. This approach is straightforward, but concatenation
    alone does not explicitly model multiplicative interactions
    between modalities.

    \item \textbf{Attention-based fusion:}
    Attention learns relationships between elements of different
    representations and uses the resulting weights to aggregate
    relevant information. Cross-modal attention has been
    demonstrated in the Multimodal Transformer for unaligned
    language, audio, and visual sequences
    \citep{tsai2019multimodal,vaswani2017attention}.

    \item \textbf{Tensor-based fusion:}
    Tensor operations explicitly represent interactions between
    modality-specific features. Tensor Fusion Networks provide
    a representative approach for modeling intra-modal and
    inter-modal dynamics in multimodal sentiment analysis
    \citep{zadeh2017tensor}. Such methods can capture higher-order
    interactions, although their computational and memory
    requirements may increase rapidly with feature dimensionality.

    \item \textbf{Graph-based fusion:}
    Modalities, objects, or observations are represented as
    nodes connected by spatial, temporal, or semantic relations.
    Graph neural networks then propagate information through
    these relationships. This mechanism is useful when the
    relational structure of the data is important
    \citep{kipf2017semi}.

    \item \textbf{Probabilistic fusion:}
    Information from different sources is combined using
    probability distributions, confidence estimates, or
    uncertainty-aware inference. This is relevant when the
    reliability of individual modalities varies, provided that
    the uncertainty estimates and model assumptions are
    appropriately validated.

    \item \textbf{Contrastive fusion:}
    Contrastive objectives encourage representations of
    corresponding observations from different modalities to
    become compatible in a shared embedding space. Image--text
    contrastive pretraining, for example, learns associations
    between visual observations and natural-language descriptions
    \citep{radford2021clip}. Contrastive learning is primarily
    a representation-learning objective rather than a unique
    fusion stage, and can be combined with attention, projection,
    or other integration mechanisms.

    \item \textbf{Generative fusion:}
    Generative models estimate, translate, or reconstruct
    information across modalities. Generative adversarial
    networks and diffusion models provide alternative approaches
    to conditional synthesis and reconstruction
    \citep{goodfellow2014generative,ho2020denoising}.
    Such methods may support incomplete-input settings, but
    synthesized information should not be assumed to reproduce
    the missing observation exactly.

    \item \textbf{Gated fusion:}
    Learnable gates regulate the contribution of different
    modality-specific representations. Gated Multimodal Units
    illustrate how multiplicative gates can determine the
    influence of individual modalities on a shared representation
    \citep{ovalle2017gated}. Gating is especially relevant when
    modality importance varies across observations.

\end{enumerate}

The categories in Table~\ref{tab:mechanisms} represent broad
analytical families rather than mutually exclusive algorithmic
classes. A model can combine contrastive learning with attention,
use a graph to organize cross-modal relationships, or incorporate
generative reconstruction into a gated fusion architecture.
Furthermore, computational cost, interpretability, and scalability
depend on implementation details, feature dimensionality, and
the evaluation setting. These properties should therefore be
assessed empirically rather than assigned as universal rankings
to an entire mechanism family
\citep{zhao2024deepfusion,li2026alignmentfusion}.


\subsection{Dimension 3: Modalities}
\label{subsec:modalities}

The modality dimension identifies the information sources processed
by a multimodal system. Modalities differ in representation,
sampling frequency, dimensionality, noise characteristics, and
semantic content. Their integration may consequently require
modality-specific encoders, projection layers, alignment procedures,
or cross-modal interaction mechanisms
\citep{baltrusaitis2019survey,jiao2024comprehensivesurvey}.

MULTIFUSION-7 considers the following principal modality categories:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Visual:}
    Images, video frames, depth maps, and other spatially
    organized visual observations. These data provide information
    about appearance, object identity, geometry, and motion.

    \item \textbf{Textual:}
    Natural-language sentences, captions, clinical notes,
    documents, and structured textual descriptions. Text
    conveys semantic content, relationships, and contextual
    information.

    \item \textbf{Audio:}
    Speech, music, and environmental sounds represented as
    waveforms, spectrograms, or learned acoustic features.
    Audio can encode linguistic content, prosody, speaker
    characteristics, and sound events.

    \item \textbf{Sensor:}
    Measurements from LiDAR, radar, GPS, inertial measurement
    units (IMUs), and other environmental or motion sensors.
    These observations support geometric perception, localization,
    motion estimation, and environmental monitoring.

    \item \textbf{Physiological:}
    Signals such as electroencephalography (EEG),
    electrocardiography (ECG), and electromyography (EMG),
    which represent neural, cardiac, and muscular activity.

    \item \textbf{Tactile:}
    Force, pressure, vibration, and contact measurements
    that describe physical interaction, surface properties,
    and contact dynamics.

    \item \textbf{Genomic and molecular:}
    DNA, RNA, protein sequences, and related molecular
    representations that support the integration of
    biological and clinical information.

\end{enumerate}

These categories describe the principal information sources,
not mutually exclusive data formats. A single application may
combine several visual or sensor subtypes, and the same modality
may be represented by raw measurements, engineered features,
or learned embeddings. For this reason, classification should
record both the modality category and the representation used
when that distinction materially affects the fusion process.

Cross-modal compatibility is another important consideration.
For example, visual and textual features may require semantic
alignment, whereas camera and LiDAR observations may require
spatial calibration. Similarly, audio and video streams may
require temporal correspondence. Recent surveys identify alignment
and representation compatibility as important considerations
in the design and evaluation of multimodal systems
\citep{baltrusaitis2019survey,li2026alignmentfusion}.


\subsection{Dimension 4: Learning Paradigm}
\label{subsec:learning_paradigm}

The learning-paradigm dimension describes the supervision signal
and optimization strategy used to train a multimodal model.
It is distinct from the fusion stage and mechanism: for example,
the same attention-based architecture can be trained using
supervised, self-supervised, or semi-supervised objectives.

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Supervised learning:}
    Model parameters are optimized using labeled examples.
    The objective directly measures the discrepancy between
    predictions and ground-truth targets. This paradigm is
    common in classification, regression, detection, and
    segmentation tasks.

    \item \textbf{Self-supervised learning:}
    Training signals are derived from the data itself rather
    than requiring a manually assigned label for every example.
    Examples include reconstruction, masked prediction, and
    cross-modal contrastive objectives. Image--text contrastive
    pretraining demonstrates how naturally paired observations
    can support transferable representations
    \citep{radford2021clip}.

    \item \textbf{Unsupervised learning:}
    Models discover structure in unlabeled data through
    objectives such as clustering, density estimation,
    or reconstruction. In multimodal systems, these methods
    can identify common structure across modalities without
    direct task labels.

    \item \textbf{Semi-supervised learning:}
    A limited labeled dataset is combined with a larger
    collection of unlabeled observations. This is useful
    when annotation is expensive but unlabeled multimodal
    data are available.

    \item \textbf{Reinforcement learning:}
    A model learns a policy or decision strategy through
    interaction with an environment and feedback in the form
    of rewards. Multimodal observations can inform state
    estimation, action selection, and feedback-driven learning.

\end{enumerate}

These paradigms are not necessarily exclusive across the complete
training pipeline. A system may first learn representations through
self-supervised pretraining and subsequently fine-tune them using
labeled examples. Likewise, supervised fusion models may use
contrastive or reconstruction objectives as auxiliary losses.
MULTIFUSION-7 therefore records the primary learning paradigm
and, where relevant, additional training objectives. This distinction
supports a more accurate comparison of methods whose architectures
may be similar but whose supervision requirements differ
\citep{baltrusaitis2019survey,jiao2024comprehensivesurvey}.


\subsection{Dimension 5: Data Condition}
\label{subsec:data_condition}

Data condition describes the availability, quality, balance, and
temporal relationship of the modalities during training and
inference. It is essential for interpreting reported performance
because models evaluated using complete, synchronized, and clean
inputs may behave differently under real-world deployment conditions.
Recent surveys explicitly examine missing modalities and low-quality
multimodal data as important challenges for robust fusion
\citep{wu2026missingmodality,zhang2026lowquality}.

MULTIFUSION-7 distinguishes five principal conditions:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Complete:}
    All required modalities are available for the observation.
    This condition provides a baseline for assessing fusion
    performance when the expected inputs are present.

    \item \textbf{Missing:}
    One or more modalities are unavailable for some observations.
    Missingness may arise from sensor failure, acquisition
    constraints, privacy restrictions, or incomplete data
    collection. Relevant strategies include modality dropout,
    missing-feature estimation, and fusion over the available
    inputs \citep{wu2026missingmodality}.

    \item \textbf{Noisy:}
    A modality is present but corrupted, degraded, or unreliable.
    Examples include blurred images, noisy audio, and inaccurate
    sensor measurements. Robust feature extraction and
    reliability-aware fusion may reduce the influence of
    degraded inputs.

    \item \textbf{Imbalanced:}
    Modalities contribute unequally to model optimization or
    prediction. One modality may dominate because it contains
    stronger task-related signals, has higher quality, or is
    easier to optimize. In this taxonomy, modality imbalance
    is distinguished from class imbalance, which concerns
    unequal frequencies of target categories.

    \item \textbf{Asynchronous:}
    Observations from different modalities are collected at
    different time steps or sampling rates and do not have
    direct temporal correspondence. This condition is
    particularly relevant to audio--visual learning and
    multisensor systems, where temporal alignment or learned
    cross-modal interactions may be required.

\end{enumerate}

These conditions should be distinguished because they represent
different failure modes. Missingness removes an information source,
noise degrades an observed source, imbalance changes its relative
influence, and asynchrony complicates correspondence between
observations. A method designed for one condition should not
automatically be considered robust to the others.

The data-condition dimension also supports more informative
evaluation. Studies should report whether all modalities are
available during training and testing, how corruption or missingness
is introduced, and whether the evaluation includes multiple
availability or quality patterns. Quality-varying inputs may be
recorded as an additional annotation when the reliability of a
modality changes across observations. These distinctions are
consistent with recent data-centric analyses of multimodal fusion
\citep{wu2026missingmodality,zhang2026lowquality}.


\subsection{Dimension 6: Backbone Architecture}
\label{subsec:taxonomy_backbone}

Backbone architecture identifies the principal neural architecture
used to extract, transform, or generate modality-specific
representations. Different architectures have different structural
properties and computational characteristics, making them suitable
for different input modalities and fusion requirements.
Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy} presents seven backbone categories
considered in MULTIFUSION-7: convolutional neural networks (CNNs),
recurrent neural networks (RNNs), graph neural networks (GNNs),
Transformers, autoencoders, generative adversarial networks (GANs),
and diffusion models. Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy_backbones} summarizes
their primary roles, strengths, and limitations.

\begin{enumerate}
    

\item\textbf{ {Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).}}
CNNs extract spatial features from images, video frames, depth maps,
and other grid-structured data. Their convolutional operations learn
hierarchical patterns, ranging from local edges and textures to
higher-level visual features. In multimodal fusion, CNNs commonly
serve as visual encoders whose outputs are combined with textual,
audio, or sensor representations. However, conventional CNNs do not
automatically establish cross-modal relationships and may require
additional attention or fusion modules to model long-range dependencies
\citep{lecun1998gradient,liu2022convnext}.

\item \textbf{{Recurrent Neural Networks and Long Short-Term Memory
(RNNs/LSTMs).}}
RNNs model sequential observations by maintaining hidden states that
summarize information from previous time steps. LSTMs extend this
approach through gating mechanisms that regulate information retention
and updating. These architectures are suitable for speech, audio,
physiological signals, and time-dependent sensor measurements.
Their sequential processing, however, can limit parallel computation,
and temporal dependencies between different modalities may still
require explicit alignment or cross-modal interaction mechanisms
\citep{hochreiter1997long}.

\item \textbf{{Graph Neural Networks (GNNs):}}
GNNs learn representations by aggregating information from connected
nodes in a graph. Nodes may represent objects, sensor observations,
image regions, or entities, while edges encode spatial, temporal,
or semantic relationships. This architecture supports relational
reasoning in applications such as autonomous driving, robotics, and
biomedical analysis. Its effectiveness depends on the quality of
the graph structure and the relevance of the relationships encoded
by its edges \citep{kipf2017semi}.

\item \textbf{{Transformers:}}
Transformers use self-attention and cross-attention to model
relationships between elements within or across modalities.
Self-attention captures dependencies within a representation,
whereas cross-attention enables one modality to selectively
aggregate information from another. The Multimodal Transformer
demonstrates the use of directional cross-modal attention for
unaligned language, audio, and visual sequences. Despite their
flexibility, Transformers can incur substantial computational costs
when processing long sequences or large numbers of multimodal tokens
\citep{vaswani2017attention,tsai2019multimodal}.

\item \textbf{{Autoencoders:}}
Autoencoders learn latent representations by encoding input data
into a lower-dimensional space and reconstructing the original
observations from those representations. Multimodal autoencoders
can learn shared features across modalities while preserving
modality-specific information. They are useful for unsupervised
representation learning, reconstruction, and research on incomplete
modalities. Nevertheless, low reconstruction error does not
necessarily imply that the learned representation preserves all
information required for a downstream task
\citep{hinton2006reducing}.

\item \textbf{Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs):}
GANs consist of a generator and a discriminator trained through
an adversarial objective. In multimodal systems, conditional
generators can translate information between modalities or estimate
a missing representation from the available inputs. These models
can support cross-modal synthesis and reconstruction, but training
may be unstable, and generated outputs may contain inaccurate
or incomplete information. Their suitability therefore depends
on both generation quality and downstream task performance
\citep{goodfellow2014generative}.

\item \textbf{{Diffusion Models:}}
Diffusion models learn to generate data by reversing a progressive
corruption process through iterative denoising. When conditioned
on other modalities, they can support cross-modal generation,
reconstruction, and missing-modality estimation. Their iterative
sampling process provides flexibility but can increase inference
time and computational requirements. Generated representations
must also be evaluated for consistency with the observed evidence
and their contribution to the target task
\citep{ho2020denoising}.
\end{enumerate}
The seven architecture categories are not mutually exclusive.
A single multimodal system may use a CNN to extract visual features,
an LSTM to encode temporal signals, and a Transformer to integrate
the resulting representations. A generative decoder may additionally
estimate information from an unavailable modality. Such architectures
should be classified according to their principal backbone while
recording additional components when they materially contribute
to the fusion process.

Within MULTIFUSION-7, backbone architecture is therefore treated
as a distinct dimension from fusion stage and fusion mechanism.
The backbone describes how modality-specific representations are
learned or generated, whereas the fusion stage identifies where
information is integrated and the fusion mechanism describes
how that integration is performed. Keeping these dimensions
separate enables a more consistent comparison of multimodal
architectures across learning paradigms, data conditions, and
application domains.

\subsection{Dimension 7: Application Domain}
\label{subsec:application_domain}

The application-domain dimension identifies the problem context
in which multimodal fusion is applied. Different domains impose
different requirements on data alignment, prediction reliability,
latency, interpretability, and the consequences of model errors.
A fusion strategy suitable for offline image classification may
not be appropriate for a real-time autonomous-driving system or
a clinical decision-support application.

MULTIFUSION-7 includes the following principal application domains:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Healthcare and biomedicine:}
    Integration of medical images, electronic health records,
    clinical notes, physiological signals, and molecular data
    for diagnosis, prognosis, patient monitoring, and clinical
    decision support.

    \item \textbf{Autonomous driving:}
    Integration of camera, LiDAR, radar, GPS, and inertial
    measurements for object detection, localization, scene
    understanding, and motion prediction.

    \item \textbf{Robotics:}
    Integration of visual perception, proprioception, tactile
    sensing, and environmental measurements for manipulation,
    navigation, and embodied interaction.

    \item \textbf{Emotion recognition:}
    Integration of facial expressions, speech, audio, and
    textual content for emotion classification, sentiment
    analysis, and affective computing.

    \item \textbf{Remote sensing:}
    Integration of optical, multispectral, hyperspectral,
    synthetic-aperture radar, and geospatial observations
    for land-cover classification, environmental monitoring,
    and change detection.

    \item \textbf{Internet of Things (IoT):}
    Integration of distributed sensor streams, environmental
    measurements, and contextual information for monitoring,
    anomaly detection, predictive maintenance, and
    intelligent control.

\end{enumerate}

These application categories represent representative areas of
multimodal research rather than an exhaustive list. A single
study may address multiple domains, and different applications
within the same domain may impose substantially different
requirements. For example, clinical decision support may prioritize
reliability and interpretability, whereas autonomous driving may
place additional emphasis on latency, spatial alignment, and
robustness to sensor failure. Consequently, application domain
should be recorded alongside the task and evaluation setting
rather than treated as a direct indicator of model performance
\citep{jiao2024comprehensivesurvey,zhao2024deepfusion,
li2026alignmentfusion}.


\subsection{Operationalization and Cross-Dimensional Comparison}
\label{subsec:taxonomy_operationalization}

The seven dimensions are intended to support consistent descriptions
of multimodal fusion methods across different research areas.
To apply MULTIFUSION-7, each study should be characterized using
evidence from its architectural description, learning objective,
input configuration, experimental setup, and application task.
The following classification principles help reduce ambiguity:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Identify the modalities:}
    Record the information sources used by the model and, when
    relevant, the representations derived from them.

    \item \textbf{Determine the fusion stage:}
    Identify where the principal integration occurs and record
    additional stages when the model employs hybrid fusion.

    \item \textbf{Identify the fusion mechanism:}
    Record the main integration operation, such as concatenation,
    attention, tensor interactions, graph propagation,
    probabilistic aggregation, contrastive alignment, generation,
    or gating. Multiple labels are appropriate when the model
    combines mechanisms.

    \item \textbf{Record the learning paradigm:}
    Distinguish the primary supervision strategy from auxiliary
    training objectives. For example, a supervised model may
    incorporate contrastive pretraining or a reconstruction loss.

    \item \textbf{Characterize the data condition:}
    Record whether the study assumes complete inputs or explicitly
    addresses missing, noisy, imbalanced, or asynchronous data.
    Training-time and inference-time conditions should be
    distinguished when they differ.

    \item \textbf{Identify the backbone architecture:}
    Record the principal encoders and any additional architectures
    that perform cross-modal interaction, reconstruction, or
    generation.

    \item \textbf{Record the application and evaluation context:}
    Identify the domain, prediction task, datasets, evaluation
    metrics, and relevant computational or reliability constraints.

\end{enumerate}

This classification process is intended to describe the assumptions
and design choices of a method, not to assign a universal ranking
to its architecture. In particular, reported performance should
be interpreted in the context of the datasets, input modalities,
training conditions, and evaluation protocols used by each study.
A method evaluated with complete and synchronized inputs cannot
be assumed to outperform another method designed for missing or
degraded modalities solely because its reported accuracy is higher.

The framework also distinguishes the proposed taxonomy from the
fusion mechanisms already established in the literature. Early,
intermediate, and late fusion remain useful descriptions of
integration stage, while attention, graph, tensor, probabilistic,
contrastive, generative, and gated approaches describe different
mechanisms or learning strategies
\citep{jiao2024comprehensivesurvey,zhao2024deepfusion,
li2026alignmentfusion}. Similarly, missingness, noise, imbalance,
and asynchrony characterize operating conditions rather than
independent architectural families
\citep{wu2026missingmodality,zhang2026lowquality}.

The principal purpose of MULTIFUSION-7 is therefore to connect
these complementary dimensions within a single descriptive
framework. By examining fusion stage and mechanism alongside
modalities, learning paradigm, data condition, backbone
architecture, and application domain, the taxonomy provides
a basis for more transparent comparisons, clearer identification
of methodological trade-offs, and more systematic discussion
of unresolved challenges in multimodal fusion.

\section{Fusion Architectures}
\label{sec:architectures}

Multimodal fusion architectures determine how information from
heterogeneous sources is integrated to support a shared prediction
or representation-learning objective. Although all fusion approaches
aim to exploit complementary information, they differ in the stage
of integration, the extent of cross-modal interaction, their
computational requirements, and their assumptions about input
availability. Traditional classifications distinguish early,
intermediate, and late fusion, while hybrid and adaptive architectures
combine multiple integration strategies or dynamically regulate
the contributions of individual modalities
\citep{atrey2010multimodal,ramachandram2017deep,
jiao2024comprehensivesurvey,zhao2024deepfusion}.

Recent research has further examined the trade-offs between these
architectural choices. Shukor et al.~\citep{shukor2025scaling}
investigated early- and late-fusion designs across native multimodal
models and different training scales, showing that architectural
performance depends on model size and experimental conditions.
Xu et al.~\citep{xu2024midfusion} demonstrated that early interaction
between audio and visual streams can introduce irrelevant
cross-modal information, motivating a messenger-guided intermediate
fusion strategy. These findings emphasize that fusion architecture
should be selected according to the structure, reliability, and
alignment of the available modalities rather than by assuming that
one fusion stage is universally superior.

\subsection{Early Fusion}
\label{subsec:early_fusion}

Early fusion integrates information from multiple modalities at the
input level or through low-level feature representations. In its
simplest form, modality-specific inputs or features are concatenated
before being processed by a shared neural network. This enables
the subsequent layers to learn relationships between modalities
from the beginning of the processing pipeline
\citep{atrey2010multimodal,ramachandram2017deep}.

Let $\mathbf{x}^{(m)}$ denote the input from modality $m$, where
$m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}$. The concatenated representation is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{early}}
    =
    \left[
    \mathbf{x}^{(1)};
    \mathbf{x}^{(2)};
    \ldots;
    \mathbf{x}^{(M)}
    \right],
    \label{eq:early_concat}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot;\cdot]$ denotes concatenation along the feature
dimension. A shared transformation then produces the fused
representation:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{early}}
    =
    f_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{early}}\right),
    \label{eq:early}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\theta}$ denotes a learnable transformation and
$\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{early}}$ is the resulting representation.
The inputs must have compatible dimensions for concatenation
along the selected axis, although modality-specific projection
layers can be used to establish this compatibility.

For example, in multimodal emotion recognition, acoustic features
and visual features extracted from facial frames may be concatenated
before a shared classifier learns associations between vocal
characteristics and facial expressions. In autonomous driving,
spatially aligned representations from camera and LiDAR sensors
may similarly be combined to exploit complementary appearance
and geometric information.

\textbf{Advantages.}
Early fusion is conceptually straightforward and permits the model
to learn cross-modal interactions throughout subsequent layers.
Joint optimization may allow the shared representation to exploit
fine-grained relationships that would be lost if each modality
were processed independently until the decision stage. Early
fusion can also be computationally efficient when the input
representations are compact and compatible.

\textbf{Limitations.}
Early fusion can be sensitive to dimensionality differences,
temporal misalignment, and modality-specific noise. Concatenation
does not itself establish semantic or temporal correspondence
between the inputs. Furthermore, a dominant or unreliable modality
may influence the shared representation disproportionately.
When a modality is missing at inference time, the architecture
may require masking, modality dropout, imputation, or a specifically
designed missing-input pathway.

Recent evidence also cautions against assuming that early fusion
is inherently inferior to late fusion. Shukor et al.
\citep{shukor2025scaling} report that early-fusion architectures
can be competitive or advantageous under particular model-scale
and training conditions. Therefore, comparisons should account
for the parameter budget, data availability, modality alignment,
and computational constraints of the evaluated systems.


\subsection{Intermediate Fusion}
\label{subsec:intermediate_fusion}

Intermediate fusion combines modality-specific representations
within one or more hidden layers of a neural network. Instead of
directly joining raw inputs, each modality is first processed by
an appropriate encoder. The resulting features are then integrated
through a fusion module that may employ concatenation, attention,
tensor interactions, graph operations, or gating
\citep{ramachandram2017deep,jiao2024comprehensivesurvey,
zhao2024deepfusion}.

Let $\mathbf{h}^{(m,l)}$ denote the representation of modality
$m$ at layer $l$. Intermediate fusion can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{fused}}^{(l)}
    =
    F^{(l)}\left(
    \mathbf{h}^{(1,l)},
    \mathbf{h}^{(2,l)},
    \ldots,
    \mathbf{h}^{(M,l)}
    \right),
    \label{eq:intermediate}
\end{equation}
where $F^{(l)}$ denotes the fusion operator applied at layer $l$.
The resulting representation may subsequently be passed through
additional shared layers or modality-specific processing blocks.

Intermediate fusion provides a compromise between early fusion,
which combines information before substantial feature extraction,
and late fusion, which primarily combines high-level predictions.
Modality-specific encoders can preserve the structural properties
of their inputs while the fusion module learns cross-modal
dependencies at a representation level appropriate to the task.

Several mechanisms are commonly used to implement intermediate
fusion:

\begin{enumerate}


    \item \textbf{Feature concatenation:}
    Encoded features are concatenated and passed through shared
    transformations. This approach is straightforward but may
    require projection layers when feature dimensions differ.

    \item \textbf{Cross-attention:}
    Representations from one modality provide queries, while
    another modality provides keys and values. The resulting
    attention operation allows one modality to select information
    relevant to its current representation.

    \item \textbf{Co-attention:}
    Attention is learned across two or more modalities so that
    their representations can inform one another. Depending on
    the architecture, the interaction may be simultaneous or
    implemented through successive attention operations.

    \item \textbf{Tensor-based interaction:}
    Modality-specific features are combined through multiplicative
    or higher-order interactions. Tensor Fusion Networks provide
    a representative approach for explicitly modeling
    intra-modal and inter-modal feature relationships
    \citep{zadeh2017tensor}.

    \item \textbf{Graph-based interaction:}
    Modality-specific observations are represented as nodes or
    connected entities, and information is propagated through
    spatial, temporal, or semantic relationships.

\end{enumerate}

Intermediate fusion is particularly useful when modalities contain
complementary information but require different feature extractors.
For example, a visual encoder can extract facial features while
an audio encoder models speech dynamics. A cross-modal attention
module can then learn which acoustic segments and facial features
are relevant to the target prediction.

However, intermediate fusion does not automatically guarantee
effective alignment. Xu et al.~\citep{xu2024midfusion} showed that
early audio--visual interaction can entangle unrelated cross-modal
context, motivating a messenger-guided mid-fusion Transformer.
This finding illustrates that intermediate fusion is not simply
a compromise between early and late fusion: its performance also
depends on the design of the interaction mechanism and the
relevance of the information exchanged.


\subsection{Late Fusion}
\label{subsec:late_fusion}

Late fusion combines high-level representations or predictions
produced by separate modality-specific models. Each model processes
its own input, and a decision-level operator integrates their
outputs to generate the final prediction. This strategy is useful
when modalities differ substantially in structure or when separate
models can be developed and evaluated independently
\citep{atrey2010multimodal,ramachandram2017deep}.

Let $f_m$ denote the predictor associated with modality $m$.
Its prediction is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{(m)}
    =
    f_m\left(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}\right),
    \label{eq:unimodal_prediction}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{(m)}$ represents a class-probability
vector or another task-specific prediction. The final prediction
is obtained using
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathbf{y}}
    =
    G\left(
    \widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{(1)},
    \widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{(2)},
    \ldots,
    \widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{(M)}
    \right),
    \label{eq:late}
\end{equation}
where $G$ denotes the decision-level fusion operator.

For classification, a weighted probability average is one possible
implementation:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{late}}
    =
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \alpha_m\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{(m)},
    \qquad
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_m=1,
    \quad \alpha_m\geq 0,
    \label{eq:late_weighted}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_m$ is the contribution assigned to modality $m$.
The weights may be fixed, learned from validation data, or
estimated dynamically. This formulation assumes that the
individual predictions are compatible, for example, that they
refer to the same class set.

\textbf{Advantage:}
Late fusion supports modular design because each modality-specific
model can be developed, pretrained, or optimized independently.
It can accommodate heterogeneous encoders and enables the
contribution of individual models to be examined separately.
When a modality becomes unavailable, a decision-level system
may continue with the remaining predictions if its aggregation
operator explicitly supports incomplete inputs.

\textbf{Limitations:}
Late fusion may lose fine-grained relationships between modality
features because the integration occurs after substantial
modality-specific processing. Its performance can also depend
on the calibration and reliability of individual predictions.
If the aggregation operator assumes that all modalities are
available, missing inputs can still cause failure.

Late fusion should not be assumed to outperform early or
intermediate fusion in every setting. The scaling study of
Shukor et al.~\citep{shukor2025scaling} found no universal
architectural advantage for late-fusion native multimodal models
over early-fusion alternatives. Consequently, the choice should
be evaluated against the task, model scale, training regime,
and operational requirements.


\subsection{Hybrid Fusion}
\label{subsec:hybrid_fusion}

Hybrid fusion combines two or more fusion stages within a single
architecture. A hybrid model may perform early or intermediate
feature integration for selected modalities and then aggregate
the resulting representations or predictions at a later stage.
This approach is particularly useful when different modality
combinations benefit from different forms of interaction
\citep{ramachandram2017deep,jiao2024comprehensivesurvey}.
For example, a multimodal perception system may integrate camera
and LiDAR features at an intermediate layer to support object
localization, while separately processing textual context and combining its output with the perception model prediction. Hybrid architectures can therefore preserve modality-specific processing where necessary while exploiting cross-modal
relationships at other stages.

A general hybrid architecture can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{hybrid}}
    =
    F_{\mathrm{late}}\left(
    F_{\mathrm{early}}\left(
    \mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)}
    \right),
    F_{\mathrm{intermediate}}\left(
    \mathbf{h}^{(3)},\mathbf{h}^{(4)}
    \right)
    \right),
    \label{eq:hybrid}
\end{equation}
where $F_{\mathrm{early}}$ and $F_{\mathrm{intermediate}}$ denote
different integration operations, and $F_{\mathrm{late}}$
combines their outputs. The intermediate inputs
$\mathbf{h}^{(3)}$ and $\mathbf{h}^{(4)}$ are modality-specific
representations produced by their respective encoders.

This expression is illustrative rather than a universal hybrid
architecture. In a practical implementation, the outputs of
different stages must be represented in compatible forms before
they can be combined by the final fusion operator.

\textbf{Advantages:}
Hybrid fusion provides architectural flexibility and allows
different modalities to interact at multiple levels. It can
combine the fine-grained interactions of feature-level fusion
with the modularity of decision-level fusion. It may also permit
different modality groups to be processed according to their
distinct structural or alignment requirements.

\textbf{Limitations:}
The additional components can increase computational cost,
memory consumption, and optimization complexity. Training may
also become more difficult when the stages have conflicting
objectives or when one modality dominates the shared representation.
The benefit of hybrid fusion must therefore be established through
controlled comparisons rather than assumed from architectural
complexity alone.

Hybrid fusion is particularly relevant to systems that combine
heterogeneous sensor streams or use modality-specific encoders
alongside shared interaction layers. Its classification should
identify which stages are combined and what information passes
between them, rather than merely labeling the architecture
as hybrid.


\subsection{Adaptive Fusion}
\label{subsec:adaptive_fusion}

Adaptive fusion dynamically adjusts the contribution of individual
modalities according to the input, context, or estimated reliability
of each source. Unlike a fixed fusion operator, an adaptive model
can assign different weights to different modalities for different
observations. This property is relevant when the usefulness of
a modality varies across samples, as occurs under changing
environmental conditions, sensor degradation, or modality
imbalance.

A general adaptive fusion function is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{adaptive}}
    =
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \alpha_m(\mathbf{x})
    \,\phi_m\left(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}\right),
    \label{eq:adaptive}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_m$ denotes the encoder for modality $m$ and
$\alpha_m(\mathbf{x})$ is an input-dependent contribution weight.
When the weights are constrained to be nonnegative and sum to
one, they can be computed using a softmax function:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_m(\mathbf{x})
    =
    \frac{\exp\left(s_m(\mathbf{x})\right)}
    {\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp\left(s_j(\mathbf{x})\right)},
    \label{eq:adaptive_softmax}
\end{equation}
where $s_m(\mathbf{x})$ is a learned score for modality $m$.
The scores may depend on modality-specific features, cross-modal
context, or an estimate of input quality. The softmax formulation
is one possible implementation; other adaptive mechanisms may
use independent gates or attention-based weighting.

\textbf{Advantages.}
Adaptive fusion can account for differences in modality relevance
across observations and reduce dependence on a single fixed
combination rule. It can also provide a mechanism for reducing
the influence of unreliable inputs when the learned weighting
function is trained and evaluated under appropriate quality
variations.

\textbf{Limitations.}
Learned weights do not automatically represent true modality
reliability or causal importance. A gating mechanism may still
over-rely on a dominant modality, particularly when the training
data exhibit modality imbalance. Moreover, adaptive weighting
alone does not recover missing information; explicit missing-input
handling may be required.

Recent studies have investigated dynamic modality assessment and
robust fusion under missing or imbalanced inputs. Bi et al.
\citep{bi2025dream} proposed DREAM, which combines sample-level
modality assessment, selective reconstruction, and soft masking
fusion. Wei et al.~\citep{wei2025arl} showed that equalizing
the contribution of all modalities is not necessarily optimal
and proposed asymmetric representation learning based on
modality-specific prediction variance. These results suggest
that adaptive fusion should account for task-relevant information
and modality quality rather than enforcing uniform contributions
by default.

Overall, early, intermediate, late, hybrid, and adaptive fusion
represent complementary architectural choices. Their relative
effectiveness depends on modality characteristics, alignment,
data quality, learning objectives, and deployment constraints.
This motivates evaluating fusion methods under consistent
experimental settings and explicitly reporting the conditions
under which a particular architecture provides an advantage.

\section{Representation Learning for Fusion}
\label{sec:representation}

Representation learning determines how information from different
modalities is encoded so that useful cross-modal relationships
can be identified and exploited. Although fusion architectures
specify where and how representations are combined, the quality
of the resulting prediction also depends on whether the learned
features preserve complementary information and establish
appropriate relationships between modalities
\citep{ramachandram2017deep,baltrusaitis2019survey}.

Two broad objectives are commonly distinguished. Joint
representation learning maps information from multiple modalities
into a shared representation space, whereas coordinated
representation learning preserves modality-specific spaces while
encouraging compatible representations for related observations.
Contrastive learning provides an important family of objectives
for learning such cross-modal relationships, although it does
not require every modality to occupy an identical representation
space.

\subsection{Joint Representation Learning}
\label{subsec:joint_representation}

Joint representation learning aims to encode different modalities
in a common feature space in which related observations have
compatible representations. Let $\phi_m$ denote the encoder
for modality $m$. Its output for observation $i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_i^{(m)}
    =
    \phi_m\left(\mathbf{x}_i^{(m)}\right).
    \label{eq:joint_encoder}
\end{equation}
A joint representation can be formed by combining the encoded
features:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_i^{\mathrm{joint}}
    =
    F\left(
    \mathbf{z}_i^{(1)},
    \ldots,
    \mathbf{z}_i^{(M)}
    \right),
    \label{eq:joint_fused}
\end{equation}
where $F$ denotes a fusion function such as concatenation,
attention, or a learned projection into a common space.

One possible training objective encourages representations of
paired observations to become similar:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}
    \sum_{(i,j,m,n)\in\mathcal{P}}
    \left\|
    \mathbf{z}_i^{(m)}
    -
    \mathbf{z}_j^{(n)}
    \right\|_2^2,
    \label{eq:joint}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}$ is a set of corresponding cross-modal
observations and the compared representations are projected
into the same dimensional space. For paired data, the indices
$i$ and $j$ refer to observations known to correspond to the
same underlying entity, event, or semantic content.

A distance-based alignment objective alone is generally
insufficient because it can encourage representational collapse,
in which different observations map to similar or identical
vectors. Practical systems therefore combine alignment with
task-specific losses, contrastive objectives, variance constraints,
or other mechanisms that preserve discriminative information.

Joint representations are useful for cross-modal retrieval,
multimodal classification, and systems that must compare information
from different sources. For example, a shared image--text embedding
space can allow a textual query to retrieve semantically related
images. However, a shared space should not be assumed to eliminate
all modality-specific information or guarantee perfect alignment.
The representation should be evaluated for both cross-modal
compatibility and downstream task performance
\citep{radford2021clip}.

Recent work further cautions that contrastive objectives do not
necessarily eliminate the modality gap. Almud\'evar et al.
\citep{almudevar2025ib} analyze multimodal alignment through the
information bottleneck perspective and show why modality-specific
information can remain in learned representations despite the
use of contrastive losses. Consequently, joint representation
learning should distinguish semantic compatibility from the
stronger assumption that all modality-specific distributions
become identical.


\subsection{Coordinated Representation Learning}
\label{subsec:coordinated_representation}

Coordinated representation learning preserves separate
modality-specific representation spaces while learning relationships
that make corresponding observations compatible. This approach
is useful when modalities differ substantially in their statistical
properties and forcing them into an identical feature distribution
could discard useful modality-specific information
\citep{baltrusaitis2019survey}.

Let $\phi_m$ denote the encoder for modality $m$, with
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}^{(m)}
    =
    \phi_m\left(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}\right).
    \label{eq:coordinated_encoder}
\end{equation}
The representations may occupy different spaces, but a similarity
function or cross-modal objective can encourage related observations
to have compatible representations. For paired examples, a
generic coordination loss is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coord}}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}
    \sum_{(i,j,m,n)\in\mathcal{P}}
    \left[
    1-
    \operatorname{sim}\left(
    q_m(\mathbf{z}_i^{(m)}),
    q_n(\mathbf{z}_j^{(n)})
    \right)
    \right],
    \label{eq:coord}
\end{equation}
where $q_m$ and $q_n$ project modality-specific representations
into a comparison space and $\operatorname{sim}$ denotes a
normalized similarity measure, such as cosine similarity.
Minimizing this objective encourages corresponding representations
to become similar after projection without requiring their original
representation spaces to be identical.

In contrast to joint representation learning, coordinated learning
allows modality-specific encoders to retain differences that may
be important for a downstream task. For example, visual features
can preserve spatial details while textual features retain
linguistic structure. The model can learn correspondence between
the two without requiring every visual and textual feature
to have the same interpretation.

However, coordination depends on the quality of the paired
observations and the definition of the alignment objective.
Overly strong alignment may suppress useful modality-specific
information, whereas weak alignment may provide insufficient
cross-modal correspondence. Recent work on multimodal contrastive
learning similarly shows that shared-space alignment and
modality-specific information must be considered together
\citep{almudevar2025ib,yi2026sharedspace}.

Joint and coordinated representation learning should therefore
be viewed as complementary design strategies. The former emphasizes
a shared representation for direct integration, whereas the latter
preserves modality-specific spaces while imposing compatibility
constraints. A practical system may combine both strategies by
learning shared features for cross-modal interactions alongside
private features for modality-specific information.


\subsection{Contrastive Learning}
\label{subsec:contrastive_learning}

Contrastive learning trains an encoder to distinguish related
observations from unrelated ones in a representation space.
In multimodal learning, positive pairs typically consist of
corresponding observations from different modalities, while
negative pairs represent observations that are not known to
correspond. This objective has become important for cross-modal
retrieval and representation learning because it can exploit
paired observations without requiring a manually assigned
task label for every example
\citep{radford2021clip,zong2025selfsupervised}.

Let $\mathbf{z}_i^{(a)}$ and $\mathbf{z}_i^{(b)}$ denote the
representations of a paired observation from modalities $a$
and $b$, respectively. A temperature-scaled contrastive loss
for an anchor from modality $a$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{a\rightarrow b}^{(i)}
    =
    -\log
    \frac{
    \exp\left(
    s(\mathbf{z}_i^{(a)},\mathbf{z}_i^{(b)})/\tau
    \right)
    }{
    \displaystyle
    \sum_{j=1}^{N}
    \exp\left(
    s(\mathbf{z}_i^{(a)},\mathbf{z}_j^{(b)})/\tau
    \right)
    },
    \label{eq:contrastive}
\end{equation}
where $s(\cdot,\cdot)$ is a similarity function, $\tau>0$
is a temperature parameter, and $N$ is the number of candidate
examples in the comparison batch. The paired observation
$\mathbf{z}_i^{(b)}$ is the positive example, while the remaining
batch examples serve as negatives under the standard paired-data
assumption.

For two modalities, a symmetric objective can be defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sym}}
    =
    \frac{1}{2N}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left(
    \mathcal{L}_{a\rightarrow b}^{(i)}
    +
    \mathcal{L}_{b\rightarrow a}^{(i)}
    \right).
    \label{eq:symmetric_contrastive}
\end{equation}
This objective encourages each modality to retrieve its
corresponding observation from the other modality. For more
than two modalities, pairwise objectives or a multi-positive
formulation can be used to represent the available cross-modal
correspondences.

Image--text contrastive pretraining, exemplified by CLIP,
demonstrates how large collections of paired observations can
support transferable cross-modal representations
\citep{radford2021clip}. Nevertheless, contrastive learning
has important limitations. The method depends on the quality
of positive pairs, and examples treated as negatives may
sometimes share relevant semantic content. Moreover, the
learned representation may retain a modality gap even when
positive pairs are drawn closer together.

~\citep{almudevar2025ib} analyze limitations
of contrastive alignment through the information bottleneck
principle, while ~\citep{yi2026sharedspace} investigate
the geometry of contrastive vision--language representations
and the role of shared-space alignment. These studies motivate
evaluating contrastive representations beyond their training
loss, including retrieval quality, transfer performance,
robustness, and the preservation of complementary information.

Contrastive learning is thus best understood as a representation-
learning objective that can be combined with different fusion
architectures. A model may use contrastive pretraining to learn
compatible modality-specific embeddings and subsequently apply
attention, concatenation, gating, or another mechanism to
produce a task-specific fused representation.

\section{Robustness in Multimodal Fusion}
\label{sec:robustness}

The practical effectiveness of multimodal fusion depends not
only on performance under ideal data conditions but also on
the ability to operate when inputs are missing, noisy, imbalanced,
or temporally misaligned. These conditions arise from sensor
failure, acquisition constraints, environmental interference,
differences in modality quality, and limitations in data collection.
A model trained and evaluated only on complete and clean inputs
may therefore provide an incomplete estimate of deployment
reliability.

Recent surveys have examined missing-modality learning and
low-quality multimodal data as distinct but related research
problems \citep{wu2026missingmodality,zhang2026lowquality}.
The healthcare-focused review by Le et al.
\citep{le2025missinghealthcare} also highlights the importance
of handling incomplete records and unavailable data sources
in clinical applications. In addition, recent conference research
has investigated robustness under sensor failure and the effects
of modality imbalance on joint optimization
\citep{deregnaucourt2025ecolaf,wei2025arl,bi2025dream}.

This section focuses on three important conditions: missing
modalities, noisy modalities, and modality imbalance. These
conditions require different treatment strategies and should
be evaluated separately rather than represented by a single
aggregate robustness score.


\subsection{Missing Modalities}
\label{subsec:missing_modalities}

Missing modalities occur when one or more expected input sources
are unavailable for a particular observation. This may result
from sensor failure, privacy restrictions, acquisition costs,
incomplete clinical records, or communication problems.
A conventional fusion model that assumes every modality is
present may fail when one of its required inputs is unavailable.
The missing-modality problem has consequently become an important
focus of multimodal learning research
\citep{wu2026missingmodality,le2025missinghealthcare}.

Let $\mathcal{A}_i$ denote the set of modalities available for
observation $i$. The fusion process can then be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_i
    =
    F\left(
    \left\{
    \phi_m(\mathbf{x}_i^{(m)})
    :m\in\mathcal{A}_i
    \right\}
    \right),
    \label{eq:missing}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_m$ is the encoder for modality $m$ and $F$ is a
fusion operator designed to process the available representations.
When all modalities are present, $\mathcal{A}_i$ contains every
modality. When inputs are missing, the system must either operate
on the available sources or estimate the missing information.

Several strategies can address this problem:

\begin{enumerate}


    \item \textbf{Imputation and reconstruction:}
    A model estimates missing information from the available
    modalities. This may use conditional autoencoders,
    generative adversarial networks, diffusion models, or
    other reconstruction mechanisms. Imputation can provide
    a complete input representation, but the estimated
    information may introduce additional uncertainty.

    \item \textbf{Modality dropout:}
    During training, selected modalities are randomly removed
    or masked to encourage the model to learn from different
    combinations of available inputs. This strategy reduces
    reliance on the assumption that all modalities are
    consistently present.

    \item \textbf{Adaptive or gated fusion:}
    The fusion module adjusts its computation according to
    the available modalities and their estimated relevance.
    This requires an architecture that can handle absent
    inputs explicitly; gating alone does not reconstruct
    information that is unavailable.

    \item \textbf{Missing-modality-invariant representation:}
    The model learns representations that preserve useful
    task-related information across different modality
    availability patterns. Such approaches can reduce
    dependence on a particular input source but may also
    discard modality-specific information if invariance
    is enforced too strongly.

    \item \textbf{Robust late fusion:}
    Modality-specific models make independent predictions,
    and a decision-level operator aggregates the outputs
    of the available models. The aggregation rule must
    be designed to remain valid when a subset of predictions
    is absent.

\end{enumerate}

Wu et al.~\citep{wu2026missingmodality} provide a recent survey
of deep multimodal learning with missing modalities, organizing
the research around methods, datasets, applications, and open
challenges. In the medical domain, Le et al.
\citep{le2025missinghealthcare} review strategies for handling
missing data and modalities in healthcare, including imputation
and fusion-based approaches. These reviews emphasize that
missingness can occur at different levels, from individual
measurements to entire modalities, and that the appropriate
response depends on the data collection process and the
downstream task.

Recent conference research has also investigated adaptive
fusion when modalities are absent or underperforming. Bi et al.
\citep{bi2025dream} propose DREAM, which assesses modality
contributions at the sample level and combines selective
reconstruction with soft-mask fusion. This illustrates how
missing-modality handling can be integrated with adaptive
weighting rather than treated as a separate preprocessing step.

Missing-modality robustness should be evaluated across different
availability patterns and missingness rates. A model that handles
the absence of one modality may still fail when several modalities
are missing simultaneously. Moreover, imputation quality and
downstream prediction quality should be reported separately,
because a plausible reconstruction does not necessarily improve
the final task.


\subsection{Noisy Modalities}
\label{subsec:noisy_modalities}

Noisy modalities are available but contain corrupted, degraded,
or unreliable information. Examples include blurred images,
background noise in speech, inaccurate sensor readings, and
corrupted physiological measurements. Unlike missingness, which
removes an input source, noise changes the information content
of an observed modality. A fusion model may consequently be
misled by an input that is present but unreliable.

Let $\mathbf{x}$ denote the multimodal input and let
$\boldsymbol{\delta}$ denote a perturbation affecting one or
more modalities. A robust training objective can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{robust}}
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mathcal{D}}
    \left[
    \max_{\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\Delta}
    \ell\left(
    f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\delta}),y
    \right)
    \right],
    \label{eq:noisy}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ denotes the data distribution, $f_{\theta}$
is the prediction model, $\ell$ is the task loss, and $\Delta$
defines the allowed perturbations. The inner maximization
represents a worst-case formulation and is appropriate when
the perturbation set models the intended threat or corruption
condition. For naturally occurring random noise, an expectation
over a specified corruption distribution may be more suitable.

Robustness can be improved through several complementary strategies:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Data augmentation and corruption training:}
    The model is trained using realistic perturbations, such
    as sensor noise, image degradation, or audio interference,
    to reduce sensitivity to expected input variations.

    \item \textbf{Reliability-aware fusion:}
    The fusion module estimates the quality or uncertainty
    of individual modalities and reduces the influence of
    unreliable representations. The estimates should be
    calibrated and validated against actual degradation.

    \item \textbf{Modality-specific regularization:}
    Separate encoders are trained to maintain useful
    representations under modality-specific corruption.
    This can reduce the propagation of errors from one
    modality to the complete fused representation.

    \item \textbf{Uncertainty-aware prediction:}
    The model represents uncertainty in its inputs or outputs
    and uses this information when combining modality-specific
    evidence. The reliability of the uncertainty estimates
    must be evaluated rather than assumed.

\end{enumerate}

Recent work on multimodal segmentation illustrates the importance
of evaluating fusion under sensor failure and noisy input
conditions. Deregnaucourt et al.~\citep{deregnaucourt2025ecolaf}
introduce an evidential late-fusion approach that reduces the
influence of conflicting modality-specific predictions. Their
experiments emphasize the importance of handling situations
in which one sensor becomes unavailable or produces evidence
that conflicts with other modalities.

The survey by Zhang et al.~\citep{zhang2026lowquality} further
distinguishes noisy, incomplete, imbalanced, and dynamically
varying multimodal data. This distinction is important because
a strategy that addresses missing inputs may not be sufficient
when all modalities are present but one is severely degraded.
Consequently, noise robustness should be measured using
well-defined corruption types and severity levels, with
comparisons against clean-input performance and unimodal baselines.


\subsection{Imbalanced Modalities}
\label{subsec:imbalanced_modalities}

Modality imbalance occurs when different modalities contribute
unequally to representation learning or prediction. This may
arise because one modality contains stronger task-relevant
signals, has higher signal quality, or is easier to optimize.
For example, a visual encoder may learn discriminative features
more quickly than an audio encoder, causing the joint model
to rely excessively on visual information. As a result, the
weaker modality may remain under-optimized despite being
potentially informative.

Distinguish modality imbalance from class imbalance.
Class imbalance refers to unequal frequencies of target labels,
whereas modality imbalance concerns differences in the learning
dynamics, predictive contribution, or reliability of input sources.
These problems may coexist but require different analyses and
interventions.

One simple adaptive weighting formulation assigns each modality
a learnable score $w_m$ and normalizes the scores using a
softmax function:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_m
    =
    \frac{\exp(w_m)}
    {\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp(w_j)},
    \label{eq:imbalanced}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_m$ denotes the normalized contribution weight.
A weighted fusion representation can then be computed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{weighted}}
    =
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \alpha_m\phi_m(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}).
    \label{eq:imbalance_weighted_fusion}
\end{equation}
This formulation provides a simple mechanism for adjusting
modality contributions, but it does not guarantee that the
learned weights correspond to modality reliability or that
the optimization imbalance has been resolved.

Several strategies have been investigated to mitigate modality
imbalance:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Adaptive gradient modulation:}
    The contribution of individual modalities to parameter
    updates is adjusted during training to reduce the tendency
    of dominant modalities to control optimization.

    \item \textbf{Modality-specific auxiliary objectives:}
    Additional losses encourage each encoder to learn
    discriminative features instead of depending exclusively
    on the joint prediction objective.

    \item \textbf{Sample-dependent weighting:}
    The contribution of a modality is adjusted according
    to the information it provides for a particular observation.
    This can accommodate cases in which different modalities
    are informative for different samples.

    \item \textbf{Data-centric balancing:}
    Training examples are selected, reweighted, or recombined
    to reduce cross-modal interference and improve learning
    from weaker modalities.

\end{enumerate}

Wei et al.~\citep{wei2025ogm} proposed On-the-fly Prediction
Modulation and On-the-fly Gradient Modulation to reduce the
under-optimization of weaker modalities. Their work demonstrates
that modality imbalance can emerge during both forward prediction
and gradient-based optimization. More recent research by Wei et al.
\citep{wei2025arl} argues that equal contributions from all
modalities are not necessarily optimal and develops asymmetric
representation learning based on modality-specific prediction
variance. Ma et al.~\citep{ma2025dataremixing} investigate
data remixing to address modality imbalance and improve
the sufficiency of unimodal representations.

These findings suggest that modality balancing should not
be equated with assigning identical weights to every source.
The appropriate contribution depends on the task-relevant
information, noise characteristics, redundancy, and statistical
properties of each modality. A reliable evaluation should
therefore report both multimodal performance and the behavior
of modality-specific models, including whether joint learning
improves or degrades the predictive contribution of individual
modalities.

In summary, robustness to missing, noisy, and imbalanced inputs
requires distinct but potentially complementary strategies.
Missing-modality methods address unavailable information,
noise-robust approaches mitigate corrupted observations, and
imbalance-aware methods address unequal contributions during
learning or inference. Evaluating these conditions separately
provides a more informative assessment of real-world multimodal
fusion than relying on performance under complete and clean
inputs alone.


\section{Evaluation and Benchmarks}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Reliable evaluation is essential for determining whether a multimodal
fusion architecture provides meaningful advantages over unimodal
models and alternative fusion strategies. Although many studies
report improvements in classification accuracy or prediction error,
these results do not necessarily establish that a model exploits
complementary information effectively, remains robust under degraded
inputs, or generalizes to previously unseen environments. A
comprehensive evaluation should therefore consider task performance,
robustness, computational efficiency, calibration, interpretability,
and reproducibility.

Multimodal evaluation is inherently application-dependent. Sentiment
analysis involves linguistic, acoustic, and visual information;
clinical prediction may combine structured electronic health records,
time-series measurements, medical images, and clinical notes;
autonomous driving integrates spatial and visual observations;
remote sensing combines complementary imaging modalities; and
robotics requires perception to support physical actions. Consequently,
the datasets, metrics, and evaluation protocols appropriate for one
application may not be sufficient for another
\citep{zadeh2018mosei,soenksen2022haim,caesar2020nuscenes,
schmitt2019sen12ms,james2020rlbench}.

Recent research has also highlighted the importance of evaluating
multimodal systems under realistic sensor failures and input-quality
variations. Liao et al.~\citep{liao2025sensorfailure} introduced
a benchmark for multimodal semantic segmentation under entire-modality
missingness, randomly missing inputs, and noisy modalities. Similarly,
Zhang et al.~\citep{zhang2026lowquality} reviewed evaluation challenges
associated with noisy, incomplete, imbalanced, and dynamically
varying multimodal data. These developments motivate an evaluation
framework that considers both predictive performance under nominal
conditions and reliability when the assumptions of complete and
high-quality input data are violated.

\subsection{Benchmarks}
\label{subsec:benchmarks}

Multimodal benchmarks provide standardized datasets, task definitions,
and evaluation protocols for comparing architectures and learning
strategies. A useful benchmark should specify the available modalities,
annotation procedure, training and test partitions, target task,
and recommended evaluation metrics. When possible, the evaluation
should follow the official dataset protocol and report results on
held-out test data.

Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} summarizes representative benchmarks
across five application domains. The selection is intended to
illustrate the diversity of multimodal evaluation settings rather
than to provide an exhaustive catalogue of available datasets.

\subsubsection{Emotion Recognition: CMU-MOSEI}

The CMU-MOSEI benchmark supports multimodal sentiment analysis
and emotion recognition using language, acoustic signals, and
visual observations. The dataset contains sentence-level video
segments with sentiment annotations and multiple emotion labels,
providing a setting in which linguistic content, vocal characteristics,
and facial expressions can contribute complementary evidence
\citep{zadeh2018mosei}.

Evaluation commonly includes binary sentiment accuracy (Acc-2),
seven-class sentiment accuracy (Acc-7), F1-score, mean absolute
error (MAE), and correlation between predicted and annotated
sentiment scores. Accuracy and F1 assess categorical predictions,
whereas MAE and correlation characterize the prediction of
continuous sentiment values. The exact binarization rule and
treatment of neutral sentiment must be specified because
different protocols can produce different results.

Recent studies continue to use CMU-MOSEI and the related
CMU-MOSI dataset to investigate cross-modal interaction,
missing-modality robustness, and reliability-aware fusion
\citep{liao2025sensorfailure}. However, performance on these
benchmarks does not necessarily establish generalization to
spontaneous conversation, different languages, or unseen
recording environments. Speaker-independent splits and consistent
feature extraction are important for meaningful comparisons.

\subsubsection{Healthcare: HAIM-MIMIC-MM}

Clinical prediction frequently requires the integration of
heterogeneous data collected at different stages of patient care.
The HAIM-MIMIC-MM framework combines information from MIMIC-IV
and linked clinical data sources, enabling evaluation using
structured patient information, time-series measurements,
clinical text, and medical images
\citep{soenksen2022haim}. MIMIC-IV itself provides electronic
health record data, while linked resources such as MIMIC-CXR
and MIMIC-IV-Note supply imaging and free-text information
\citep{johnson2023mimiciv}.

Clinical prediction tasks may include mortality prediction,
diagnosis, readmission prediction, and other patient-level
risk assessments. AUROC measures the ability to distinguish
positive and negative outcomes across decision thresholds,
while AUPRC is particularly informative when positive outcomes
are rare. F1-score provides a threshold-dependent balance
between precision and recall. Calibration measures are also
important because a clinically useful model should produce
probabilities that correspond reasonably well to observed
outcome frequencies.

The HAIM framework demonstrated the evaluation of numerous
combinations of clinical data sources and modalities across
multiple predictive tasks \citep{soenksen2022haim}. This provides
a useful example of why modality ablation is important: performance
should be compared using individual modalities, relevant modality
combinations, and the complete multimodal configuration.

Clinical benchmarks require additional caution concerning patient
overlap, temporal leakage, missing observations, and differences
between institutions. All records belonging to a patient should
be handled consistently across data partitions, and the temporal
availability of each input should match the intended prediction
time. Furthermore, gains on a retrospective dataset do not
establish clinical utility without external validation and
appropriate prospective evaluation.

\subsubsection{Autonomous Driving: nuScenes}

The nuScenes benchmark provides synchronized multimodal observations
from cameras, LiDAR, and radar for autonomous-driving research.
It contains annotated driving scenes and supports 3D object
detection and tracking, enabling comparisons between individual
sensor modalities and their fused representations
\citep{caesar2020nuscenes}.

For 3D object detection, mean average precision (mAP) measures
detection performance across object classes, while the nuScenes
Detection Score (NDS) combines average precision with several
true-positive error measures. These include translation,
scale, orientation, velocity, and attribute errors. Reporting
NDS alongside its component metrics provides a more informative
assessment than reporting mAP alone, because two methods with
similar detection accuracy may differ in localization or
motion-estimation quality.

The official nuScenes evaluation protocol should be followed
when comparing detection systems. Results should also identify
the sensor configuration, use of external training data,
inference speed, and other implementation conditions that may
affect comparison. In practical deployment, robustness to
sensor failure, adverse weather, and degraded observations
is also important. The recent benchmark of Liao et al.
\citep{liao2025sensorfailure} illustrates the value of
evaluating missing and noisy modalities explicitly, although
its semantic-segmentation setting is distinct from the
3D-detection task of nuScenes.

\subsubsection{Remote Sensing: SEN12MS}

Remote sensing provides a natural setting for multimodal fusion
because different sensors capture complementary information
about the Earth's surface. SEN12MS contains corresponding
Sentinel-1 synthetic-aperture radar (SAR) observations,
Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery, and land-cover labels,
supporting research on multimodal representation learning
and land-cover mapping \citep{schmitt2019sen12ms}.

SAR imagery provides information related to radar backscatter
and surface structure, whereas optical multispectral imagery
captures spectral characteristics of the observed scene.
Their integration can be useful when the information supplied
by one sensor complements that of the other. However, differences
in sensor resolution, acquisition conditions, geolocation,
and observation quality must be considered when constructing
training and evaluation partitions.

For land-cover classification and segmentation, macro-F1
summarizes class-wise precision and recall, while intersection
over union (IoU) measures the overlap between predicted and
ground-truth regions. Mean IoU (mIoU) averages IoU across
classes and is widely used for segmentation. Class-wise metrics
are important when land-cover categories occur at substantially
different frequencies.

Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 are satellite missions, whereas
SEN12MS is a specific dataset constructed from their observations.
This distinction should be maintained when describing the
benchmark. Geographic separation between training and test
regions, consistent label definitions, and careful handling
of spatially correlated patches are also important for
estimating generalization to unseen areas.

\subsubsection{Robotics: RLBench}

RLBench is a simulated robot-learning benchmark containing
100 designed manipulation tasks and a range of visual and
proprioceptive observations. Its visual observations include
RGB, depth, and segmentation information, while proprioceptive
observations describe the robot's internal state. The benchmark
also provides demonstrations that support imitation learning,
reinforcement learning, and multi-task learning
\citep{james2020rlbench}.

For manipulation tasks, success rate measures the proportion
of evaluation episodes in which the specified task objective
is completed. When multiple tasks are considered, average
success rate summarizes performance across tasks. Individual
task results should also be reported because an aggregate
score may conceal failure on particular tasks or under
specific configurations.

Robotics evaluation differs from static classification because
the model must use its observations to generate actions that
affect the environment. Evaluation should therefore consider
task completion, consistency across object configurations,
number of demonstrations, and computational requirements.
Simulation results should not automatically be interpreted
as evidence of equivalent real-world performance, because
visual appearance, dynamics, and sensor characteristics can
differ between simulated and physical environments.

\subsubsection{Benchmark Selection and Comparability}

The benchmarks summarized in Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} represent
different evaluation problems and should not be treated as
directly interchangeable. CMU-MOSEI evaluates sentiment and
emotion prediction, HAIM-MIMIC-MM supports clinical prediction,
nuScenes evaluates driving perception, SEN12MS supports remote
sensing analysis, and RLBench measures task execution in
robotic environments. Each requires metrics aligned with its
prediction target and operational requirements.

For this reason, a rigorous multimodal evaluation should document
the dataset version, data partition, input modalities, preprocessing
procedure, model selection protocol, and evaluation metrics.
The use of a common benchmark alone does not guarantee a fair
comparison if methods use different data splits, external
pretraining resources, modality availability assumptions,
or evaluation rules. Recent work on low-quality multimodal
data and sensor-failure benchmarking reinforces the need to
report the conditions under which a model is evaluated
\citep{zhang2026lowquality,liao2025sensorfailure}.


\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:evaluation_metrics}

A comprehensive evaluation of multimodal fusion should distinguish
task performance from robustness, computational efficiency,
calibration, and interpretability. These dimensions answer
different questions and should be reported separately whenever
possible. A model may achieve high accuracy while being poorly
calibrated, computationally expensive, or sensitive to the
loss of one input modality.

\subsubsection{Task Performance Metrics}

Task performance metrics quantify the quality of predictions
relative to ground-truth annotations. The choice of metric
depends on the target task, the distribution of labels,
and the cost of different types of errors.

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
For classification, accuracy is the proportion of correctly
classified examples:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Accuracy}
    =
    \frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN},
    \label{eq:metric_accuracy}
\end{equation}
where $TP$, $TN$, $FP$, and $FN$ denote true positives,
true negatives, false positives, and false negatives,
respectively. Accuracy is intuitive but can be misleading
when class frequencies are highly imbalanced.

\paragraph{Precision, recall, and F1-score.}
Precision measures the proportion of predicted positive
examples that are correct, while recall measures the proportion
of actual positive examples that are identified:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Precision}
    =
    \frac{TP}{TP+FP},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{Recall}
    =
    \frac{TP}{TP+FN}.
    \label{eq:metric_precision_recall}
\end{equation}
The F1-score is their harmonic mean:
\begin{equation}
    F_1
    =
    2\,
    \frac{\mathrm{Precision}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}}
    {\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
    \label{eq:metric_f1}
\end{equation}
Macro-F1 averages the F1-score across classes, giving each
class equal weight, whereas micro-F1 aggregates decisions
across classes before calculating the score. The averaging
convention should be reported explicitly for imbalanced
multiclass tasks.

\paragraph{Area under the ROC and precision--recall curves.}
The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve
(AUROC) measures ranking discrimination across decision
thresholds. AUPRC summarizes the precision--recall trade-off
and is particularly useful when positive examples are rare.
Neither metric alone establishes that predicted probabilities
are well calibrated or that a model is useful at a particular
operating threshold.

\paragraph{Mean absolute error and correlation.}
For continuous prediction tasks, mean absolute error (MAE)
measures the average magnitude of prediction errors:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MAE}
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left|\widehat{y}_i-y_i\right|,
    \label{eq:metric_mae}
\end{equation}
where $y_i$ is the ground-truth value and $\widehat{y}_i$
is the predicted value. Correlation measures the strength
of association between predictions and targets, but a high
correlation does not necessarily imply low prediction error.

\paragraph{Mean average precision and intersection over union.}
In object detection, mean average precision (mAP) summarizes
average precision across the evaluated classes, following
the benchmark-specific matching and threshold rules. In
segmentation, intersection over union is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{IoU}
    =
    \frac{|P\cap G|}{|P\cup G|},
    \label{eq:metric_iou}
\end{equation}
where $P$ and $G$ denote the predicted and ground-truth
regions, respectively. Mean IoU averages the class-wise
IoU values under the specified evaluation protocol.

\paragraph{Task success rate.}
In robotics, success rate measures the proportion of episodes
in which the agent satisfies the task's success criteria:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SuccessRate}
    =
    \frac{N_{\mathrm{successful}}}{N_{\mathrm{episodes}}}.
    \label{eq:metric_success}
\end{equation}
For multi-task evaluation, the average across tasks should
be accompanied by per-task results when possible.

These metrics should be selected according to the application
and reported using the definitions required by the corresponding
benchmark. For example, CMU-MOSEI requires sentiment-specific
metrics, nuScenes uses its defined detection protocol, and
RLBench evaluates task execution rather than static classification
\citep{zadeh2018mosei,caesar2020nuscenes,james2020rlbench}.


\subsubsection{Robustness Metrics}

Robustness measures how performance changes when the input
conditions differ from the nominal evaluation setting. Relevant
conditions include missing modalities, noisy measurements,
sensor failure, temporal misalignment, and distribution shift.
Liao et al.~\citep{liao2025sensorfailure} distinguish entire-modality
missingness, randomly missing inputs, and noisy modalities in
multimodal semantic segmentation. Zhang et al.
\citep{zhang2026lowquality} further discuss incomplete,
imbalanced, noisy, and dynamically varying multimodal data.

Let $S_{\mathrm{clean}}$ denote a metric for which higher values
indicate better performance under clean inputs, and let
$S_{\mathrm{condition}}$ denote the corresponding score under
a specified corruption or missing-modality condition. A relative
performance-drop measure is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{robust}}
    =
    \frac{
    S_{\mathrm{clean}}-S_{\mathrm{condition}}
    }{
    |S_{\mathrm{clean}}|+\epsilon
    },
    \label{eq:metric_robustness}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon>0$ prevents division by zero. A smaller
nonnegative value indicates a smaller relative drop for a
higher-is-better metric. When the baseline score is close
to zero, the relative drop may be unstable and should be
supplemented with absolute performance differences.

Robustness evaluation should report results for individual
missing or corrupted modalities, multiple severity levels,
and combinations of failure conditions when appropriate.
The missing-modality setting should also specify whether
the model has encountered missing inputs during training.
These distinctions are necessary because robustness to a
missing modality does not automatically imply robustness
to a present but misleading modality.

\subsubsection{Computational Efficiency}

Computational efficiency is important when multimodal systems
must operate under limited memory, power, or latency budgets.
Relevant measures include the number of model parameters,
floating-point operations (FLOPs), peak memory consumption,
inference latency, throughput, and, where measurable, energy
consumption.

These measures capture different properties. FLOPs estimate
computational workload but do not directly determine wall-clock
latency. Inference latency depends on hardware, batch size,
implementation, input resolution, and sequence length. Parameter
count measures model size but does not account for all
activation-memory requirements. Accordingly, efficiency
comparisons should report the hardware and software environment,
batch size, input configuration, and measurement procedure.

For real-time autonomous driving or robotics, latency and
throughput may be as important as predictive performance.
For clinical applications, computational requirements should
be assessed alongside reliability and calibration. A useful
comparison therefore reports task performance and computational
cost together rather than treating the most accurate model
as automatically the most practical solution.

\subsubsection{Calibration and Uncertainty}

Calibration assesses whether predicted confidence values
correspond to observed correctness frequencies. This is
important when the confidence of a prediction affects
downstream decisions, particularly in healthcare and
safety-critical perception.

For example, the expected calibration error (ECE) can be
estimated by dividing predictions into $K$ confidence bins:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{ECE}
    =
    \sum_{k=1}^{K}
    \frac{|B_k|}{N}
    \left|
    \mathrm{acc}(B_k)
    -
    \mathrm{conf}(B_k)
    \right|,
    \label{eq:metric_ece}
\end{equation}
where $B_k$ is the set of predictions in bin $k$,
$\mathrm{acc}(B_k)$ is the observed accuracy within the bin,
and $\mathrm{conf}(B_k)$ is the mean predicted confidence.
Lower ECE generally indicates better calibration under the
selected binning scheme, although ECE depends on the number
and definition of the bins.

Other measures, such as the Brier score and negative log-likelihood,
can complement ECE. Calibration should be assessed on clean
and degraded inputs when reliability under changing data
conditions is relevant. In multimodal systems, it can also
be informative to examine whether uncertainty estimates
identify unreliable modalities or appropriately reflect
uncertainty in the final prediction.


\subsubsection{Interpretability and Explainability}

Interpretability concerns whether researchers and users can
understand how a multimodal system reaches its predictions
and how individual modalities contribute to the output.
Potential analysis methods include modality ablation, feature
attribution, counterfactual interventions, attention
visualization, and comparisons between predictions obtained
with different input combinations.

Attention weights can help visualize which representations
interact within an attention-based model, but they should not
automatically be treated as faithful explanations of model
behavior. Similarly, feature-attribution methods may depend
on the choice of baseline, perturbation procedure, or
explanation algorithm. Their usefulness should be assessed
through appropriate validation rather than inferred from
the availability of a visualization alone.

For multimodal fusion, modality ablation provides a direct
way to examine how predictions change when an input source
is removed. However, the effect of removing a modality does
not necessarily measure its independent contribution because
modalities may be redundant or interact nonlinearly. Explanation
results should therefore be interpreted alongside task
performance, robustness tests, and the characteristics of
the data.

\subsubsection{Reproducibility and Fair Comparison}

A reliable benchmark comparison requires consistent data
partitions, preprocessing, training protocols, and metric
definitions. Studies should report the dataset version,
modality configuration, training and validation procedure,
hyperparameter-selection strategy, random seeds, and
computational environment. When possible, code and evaluation
scripts should be made available to facilitate independent
reproduction.

Unimodal baselines and modality-ablation experiments are
particularly important. A multimodal model should be compared
with models trained using individual modalities under clearly
specified experimental conditions. Such comparisons help
determine whether the observed benefit comes from complementary
information or from differences in model capacity, pretraining,
or optimization.

Where computationally feasible, results should be reported
across multiple random seeds with suitable measures of
variability, such as standard deviations or confidence
intervals. Statistical comparisons should use a procedure
appropriate to the data and experimental design. Differences
in reported scores should not automatically be interpreted
as statistically meaningful improvements.

Finally, benchmark results should be interpreted in the context
of the task and operating conditions. A model that performs
well on a single benchmark may still be sensitive to changes
in domain, modality quality, or input availability. Combining
task metrics with robustness, efficiency, calibration, and
reproducibility assessments provides a more complete basis
for comparing multimodal fusion architectures
\citep{zhang2026lowquality,liao2025sensorfailure}.


\subsection{Recommendations for Multimodal Evaluation}
\label{subsec:evaluation_recommendations}

Based on the benchmarks and metrics discussed above, we recommend
the following practices when evaluating multimodal fusion systems:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Use task-appropriate metrics:}
    Select metrics that reflect the prediction objective and
    report their exact definitions, averaging conventions,
    and decision thresholds.

    \item \textbf{Include unimodal baselines:}
    Compare the complete fusion model against relevant
    modality-specific models to establish whether multimodal
    integration provides a measurable benefit.

    \item \textbf{Perform modality ablations:}
    Evaluate selected modality combinations to determine
    whether individual sources provide complementary
    information or introduce redundancy and interference.

    \item \textbf{Evaluate realistic data failures:}
    Test missing, noisy, imbalanced, and asynchronous
    conditions separately where applicable. Specify the
    corruption procedure, missingness pattern, and severity.

    \item \textbf{Report efficiency and reliability:}
    Include inference latency, model size, and memory usage
    when computational constraints matter. Add calibration
    or uncertainty measures when prediction confidence
    affects downstream decisions.

    \item \textbf{Ensure reproducibility:}
    Follow official benchmark splits, document preprocessing
    and training protocols, and report uncertainty in the
    experimental results whenever feasible.

    \item \textbf{Assess generalization:}
    Where data permit, evaluate on independent subjects,
    geographic regions, institutions, or environments
    to examine performance beyond the training distribution.

\end{enumerate}

These recommendations support a more comprehensive evaluation
of multimodal fusion than task accuracy alone. They also align
with the central objective of MULTIFUSION-7: comparing fusion
methods in relation to their architecture, modalities, learning
assumptions, data conditions, and application requirements.


\section{Applications}
\label{sec:applications}

Multimodal fusion has become an important component of deep learning
systems that operate on heterogeneous data sources. By integrating
complementary information from different modalities, fusion models
can represent complex phenomena more comprehensively than approaches
that rely exclusively on a single input source. However, the benefits
of multimodal integration depend on the relevance, quality, alignment,
and availability of the input data, as well as on the architecture
used to combine their representations
\citep{baltrusaitis2019survey,jiao2024comprehensivesurvey,
zhao2024deepfusion}.

The practical requirements of fusion vary substantially across
application domains. Healthcare emphasizes clinically meaningful
predictions, data privacy, and reliable uncertainty estimates;
autonomous driving requires accurate perception under changing
environmental conditions; robotics combines perception with
physical interaction and action; emotion recognition integrates
behavioral and linguistic cues; and remote sensing exploits
complementary observations of the Earth's surface. Recent surveys
in these areas highlight the continuing importance of cross-modal
alignment, robustness to incomplete inputs, computational efficiency,
and generalization beyond the training distribution
\citep{krones2025healthcare,qian2025driving,
han2026robotvision,moon2026emotion,zhang2026opticalsar}.

The following subsections examine representative applications,
the modalities they integrate, the fusion strategies commonly
employed, and the principal challenges that remain.


\subsection{Healthcare and Biomedicine}
\label{subsec:healthcare}

Healthcare is an important application domain for multimodal
fusion because clinical decisions frequently depend on several
complementary sources of evidence. These sources include medical
images, electronic health records (EHRs), laboratory measurements,
physiological time series, clinical notes, and genomic or molecular
data. Each source provides a different perspective on a patient's
condition, and their integration can support diagnosis, prognosis,
risk stratification, disease monitoring, and treatment planning
\citep{teoh2024healthcare,krones2025healthcare,
lin2025healthcare}.

Medical imaging provides spatial information about anatomical
structures and pathological changes, whereas structured EHR data
contain demographic information, diagnoses, medications, laboratory
results, and vital signs. Clinical notes contribute unstructured
descriptions of symptoms, medical history, and clinical assessments.
Genomic data can provide additional information about biological
variation and disease mechanisms. Combining these sources may
enable a model to exploit complementary evidence that is not
fully represented by any individual modality.

Different fusion strategies support different clinical tasks.
Early fusion can combine structured clinical variables or
projected modality-specific features before joint processing.
Intermediate fusion can integrate image embeddings, textual
representations, and time-series features through attention
or shared latent representations. Late fusion can combine
predictions from separate modality-specific models, which may
be useful when data sources have different acquisition procedures
or availability patterns. Krones et al.~\citep{krones2025healthcare}
review multimodal machine learning approaches across clinical
data types, datasets, and stages of model development, while
Teoh et al.~\citep{teoh2024healthcare} examine fusion techniques
for medical images, structured data, and unstructured clinical
information.

Multimodal fusion is particularly relevant to clinical prediction
tasks in which different sources describe complementary aspects
of patient status. For example, a model may combine a chest
radiograph with structured clinical measurements and a clinical
report to estimate a disease-related outcome. The imaging
representation captures visual abnormalities, structured
measurements provide quantitative clinical context, and the
report contributes diagnostic language and contextual evidence.
Such a system must ensure that all inputs correspond to the
appropriate patient and prediction time.

Recent research has also examined electronic health record-driven
multimodal representation learning. Dou et al.~\citep{dou2026ehr}
survey methods that combine clinical narratives, imaging,
physiological waveforms, and laboratory measurements. Their
analysis highlights the importance of representation learning
and the need to account for the heterogeneous structure of
clinical data. Multimodal medical image fusion is another
active area, with recent reviews examining the integration
of MRI, CT, PET, SPECT, and other imaging modalities for
diagnosis and segmentation
\citep{li2024medicalfusion,zubair2025medicalfusion}.

Despite these opportunities, clinical fusion presents substantial
challenges. Missing data are common because different examinations
are not performed for every patient, and the absence of a modality
may itself reflect clinical decisions or patient characteristics.
Models must therefore distinguish genuine missingness from
unobserved information and avoid introducing systematic bias
through imputation. Data leakage, differences between institutions,
privacy constraints, and limited external validation can also
produce overly optimistic estimates of clinical performance.

Consequently, healthcare fusion systems should be evaluated using
patient-independent data partitions, clinically appropriate metrics,
calibration analysis, and external validation where possible.
Predictive improvements alone do not establish clinical utility;
the reliability, interpretability, fairness, and operational
suitability of the resulting system must also be considered
\citep{krones2025healthcare,lin2025healthcare}.


\subsection{Autonomous Driving}
\label{subsec:autonomous_driving}

Autonomous driving requires reliable perception of dynamic
environments containing vehicles, pedestrians, road boundaries,
traffic signals, and other objects. Because each sensing
technology has different strengths and limitations, modern
perception systems integrate information from cameras, light
detection and ranging (LiDAR), radar, and localization sensors
such as global positioning systems (GPS) and inertial measurement
units (IMUs). These modalities provide complementary information
about appearance, geometry, distance, motion, and vehicle state
\citep{caesar2020nuscenes,qian2025driving,
viktor2026sensorfusion}.

Camera images provide rich color, texture, and semantic information
that supports object recognition and scene understanding. LiDAR
provides three-dimensional geometric measurements, whereas radar
provides range and relative-velocity information and can remain
useful under certain adverse visibility conditions. GPS and IMU
measurements support localization and motion estimation. The
integration of these sources can reduce dependence on the
limitations of an individual sensor, provided that their
measurements are aligned and their reliability is assessed.

Fusion architectures are commonly organized into early,
intermediate, and late integration. Early fusion combines
sensor-level observations or low-level representations, while
intermediate fusion integrates learned features through
attention, geometric projection, or shared spatial representations.
Late fusion combines predictions from separate sensor-specific
models. Bird's-eye-view (BEV) representations provide another
important design strategy by mapping information from different
sensors into a common spatial representation that facilitates
object detection, tracking, and scene understanding
\citep{qian2025driving,ping2026bev}.

The nuScenes benchmark provides a widely used evaluation setting
for multimodal autonomous-driving perception, including camera,
LiDAR, and radar data \citep{caesar2020nuscenes}. Tasks include
three-dimensional object detection, tracking, and related
perception problems. Mean average precision (mAP), the nuScenes
Detection Score (NDS), and localization and velocity errors
are commonly used to assess detection quality and geometric
accuracy.

Recent surveys have examined the evolution of deep learning-based
sensor fusion, including BEV-centric architectures, cross-modal
attention, and methods for integrating heterogeneous sensor
measurements. Qian et al.~\citep{qian2025driving} discuss
fusion strategies, benchmark evaluation, and real-time deployment
constraints. Viktor and Kiss~\citep{viktor2026sensorfusion}
further review sensing technologies, uncertainty-aware fusion,
and safety-related challenges in autonomous vehicles.

A major challenge is that sensor measurements can become
unreliable because of weather, occlusion, calibration errors,
synchronization drift, or hardware failure. A model that performs
well under normal conditions may therefore degrade when one
sensor produces misleading information or becomes unavailable.
In addition, complex fusion architectures may impose substantial
latency and memory requirements, which can limit their suitability
for real-time operation.

Future progress requires evaluation under realistic sensor
failure and environmental conditions, together with computational
efficiency and uncertainty-aware perception. Robust autonomous
driving systems should not only achieve high average detection
accuracy but also identify unreliable inputs and degrade safely
when expected information is unavailable
\citep{qian2025driving,viktor2026sensorfusion}.


\subsection{Robotics and Embodied AI}
\label{subsec:robotics}

Robotics and embodied artificial intelligence require models
to connect perception with physical interaction and action.
Unlike systems that operate exclusively on static data,
robots observe their environment, execute actions, and receive
new sensory information as the environment changes. Multimodal
fusion is therefore important for integrating visual observations,
language instructions, proprioception, tactile measurements,
and other sensor information to support manipulation, navigation,
and human--robot interaction
\citep{james2020rlbench,zhao2025robotinteraction,
han2026robotvision}.

Visual inputs provide information about object appearance,
position, and scene structure. Proprioceptive measurements
describe the robot's joint positions, velocities, and internal
configuration, while tactile and force sensors provide information
about contact, pressure, and interaction dynamics. Language
instructions can specify task goals or constraints that are
not directly observable from physical sensors. Integrating
these modalities enables a robot to relate a desired action
to the observed state of its environment.

For example, a manipulation system may use a camera to locate
an object, proprioception to estimate the robot's configuration,
and tactile feedback to determine whether the object has been
grasped securely. Visual and proprioceptive representations
can be combined before action prediction, while tactile feedback
can be incorporated during interaction to update the robot's
estimate of contact state. This illustrates the importance of
both cross-modal feature integration and temporal feedback
in embodied systems.

Fusion strategies depend on the task and control architecture.
Early fusion may combine synchronized sensor features, whereas
intermediate fusion can use cross-attention or shared latent
representations to integrate visual, tactile, and proprioceptive
information. Late fusion may combine task-specific predictions
or state estimates. Vision--language models provide an additional
mechanism for connecting visual observations with natural-language
instructions, although their integration into physical control
systems introduces further requirements involving grounding,
latency, and action reliability
\citep{zhao2025robotinteraction,han2026robotvision}.

RLBench provides a simulated environment for evaluating robotic
manipulation and multi-task learning, with visual and
proprioceptive observations and demonstrations
\citep{james2020rlbench}. Such benchmarks support controlled
experiments across different tasks and configurations.
However, performance in simulation does not necessarily
translate directly to real-world deployment because sensor
characteristics, object dynamics, friction, and environmental
conditions may differ.

A central challenge is maintaining consistent representations
across sensing, planning, and action. Sensor delays, tactile
noise, visual occlusion, and changes in object configuration
can cause the fused representation to become inconsistent
with the actual physical state. Furthermore, a perception
model may produce a plausible scene representation without
providing sufficiently accurate information for safe control.

Recent robotics surveys emphasize the integration of multimodal
perception with decision-making and vision--language-based
robotic capabilities \citep{zhao2025robotinteraction,
han2026robotvision}. Future systems should combine robust
cross-modal alignment with temporal reasoning, efficient
inference, and closed-loop evaluation. Such evaluation should
consider task success, robustness to sensor degradation,
generalization to unseen objects, and performance in physical
environments rather than relying solely on static perception
metrics.


\subsection{Emotion Recognition}
\label{subsec:emotion_recognition}

Emotion recognition aims to infer affective states from observable
behavior and contextual information. Human emotion is expressed
through multiple channels, including linguistic content,
vocal characteristics, facial expressions, and body movements.
Consequently, multimodal emotion recognition integrates textual,
audio, and visual information to capture complementary affective
cues that may be ambiguous or incomplete when considered
individually \citep{zadeh2018mosei,zhang2024mer,
moon2026emotion}.

Textual representations capture semantic content and word choice.
Audio representations describe prosody, pitch, intensity,
speech rhythm, and other acoustic characteristics, while visual
representations capture facial expressions, head movements,
and other observable behavior. These cues may reinforce
one another or convey different information. For example,
the linguistic content of an utterance may appear positive
while its vocal delivery and facial expression suggest
sarcasm or frustration.

Early fusion can combine extracted text, audio, and visual
features before shared processing. Intermediate fusion can
use cross-modal attention or Transformer-based architectures
to learn relationships between words, acoustic segments,
and visual frames. Late fusion can combine modality-specific
emotion predictions, while adaptive fusion can adjust
the influence of a modality according to its relevance
or reliability. The Multimodal Transformer for unaligned
language sequences illustrates how cross-modal attention
can model relationships between language, audio, and
visual streams without requiring strict temporal alignment
\citep{tsai2019multimodal}.

CMU-MOSEI is a representative benchmark for multimodal
sentiment analysis and emotion recognition, providing
language, audio, and visual information with sentiment
and emotion annotations \citep{zadeh2018mosei}. Related
benchmarks, including CMU-MOSI and IEMOCAP, support
evaluation under different annotation schemes and conversational
settings. These datasets are useful for comparing fusion
architectures, but differences in label definitions, data
partitions, and evaluation protocols must be considered
when interpreting reported results.

Recent surveys examine Transformer-based fusion, modality-specific
representation learning, adaptive gating, and robustness
in multimodal emotion recognition. Zhang et al.
\citep{zhang2024mer} review deep learning-based methods
for integrating audio, visual, and textual modalities.
Moon et al.~\citep{moon2026emotion} further examine
research published between 2021 and 2025, including
pretrained encoders, self-supervised learning, and
challenges associated with alignment, missing inputs,
generalization, and interpretability.

Despite these advances, emotion recognition remains challenging
because affective expression varies across individuals, cultures,
contexts, and recording conditions. Audio may be corrupted
by background noise, facial expressions may be occluded,
and textual content may not reflect the speaker's actual
emotional state. Models may also learn dataset-specific
correlations rather than generalizable affective patterns.

Accordingly, emotion recognition systems should be evaluated
under speaker-independent and, where possible, cross-corpus
settings. Robustness to missing or noisy modalities, performance
across different demographic groups, and the interpretability
of the learned affective representations are also important.
Recent surveys identify cross-corpus generalization, lightweight
architectures, and reliable multimodal alignment as continuing
research challenges \citep{zhang2024mer,moon2026emotion}.


\subsection{Remote Sensing}
\label{subsec:remote_sensing}

Remote sensing provides observations of the Earth's surface
through multiple sensing technologies, including optical
imagery, multispectral imagery, hyperspectral measurements,
and synthetic aperture radar (SAR). These modalities capture
different physical properties and can provide complementary
information for land-cover classification, change detection,
environmental monitoring, agricultural assessment, and
disaster management
\citep{schmitt2019sen12ms,zhang2026opticalsar}.

Optical imagery captures reflected electromagnetic radiation
and provides spectral and visual information about surface
materials. Multispectral imagery records measurements across
selected spectral bands, while hyperspectral imagery provides
more densely sampled spectral information. SAR uses active
microwave sensing to measure backscatter related to surface
structure, roughness, and dielectric properties. Because SAR
and optical imagery respond differently to environmental
conditions, their integration can improve scene interpretation
when their observations are appropriately aligned.

Early fusion may concatenate spectral bands or sensor-specific
features, while intermediate fusion can combine learned
representations through attention, convolutional operations,
or shared latent spaces. Late fusion can combine independent
predictions from optical and SAR models. The appropriate
strategy depends on the target task, spatial resolution,
acquisition timing, and quality of the available observations.

SEN12MS provides paired Sentinel-1 SAR and Sentinel-2
multispectral observations and is a representative dataset
for multimodal remote sensing research
\citep{schmitt2019sen12ms}. Such datasets enable researchers
to investigate the complementary contribution of radar
and optical information in land-cover classification and
related Earth-observation tasks.

Recent research has continued to examine the theoretical
and practical aspects of optical--SAR fusion. Zhang et al.
\citep{zhang2026opticalsar} review the complementarity
of optical and SAR imagery, the evolution of fusion methods,
available datasets, and opportunities for more generalizable
and computationally efficient systems. Their discussion
highlights the importance of integrating complementary
information while preserving modality-specific characteristics.

Remote sensing fusion also faces substantial challenges.
Optical imagery can be affected by cloud cover, atmospheric
conditions, and illumination changes, whereas SAR measurements
may contain speckle and exhibit complex scattering patterns.
Temporal differences between acquisitions, spatial
misregistration, resolution differences, and inconsistent
land-cover annotations can further complicate fusion.

Evaluation should therefore consider not only classification
or segmentation performance but also generalization to
unseen geographic regions, seasonal changes, and variations
in acquisition conditions. Metrics such as macro-F1,
mean intersection over union, and class-wise accuracy
are useful for classification and segmentation, while
change-detection tasks require metrics appropriate to
the identification of changed regions. Spatially independent
test partitions are particularly important because nearby
image patches may share substantial geographic information.

Future work should investigate robust cross-sensor alignment,
uncertainty-aware fusion, efficient architectures, and
generalization across regions and acquisition conditions.
These directions are important for developing remote sensing
systems that remain reliable when individual modalities
are incomplete, noisy, or acquired under different
environmental conditions
\citep{zhang2026opticalsar,zhang2026lowquality}.


\subsection{Cross-Domain Synthesis}
\label{subsec:application_synthesis}

The application domains discussed above share a common
motivation: heterogeneous modalities provide complementary
information that can improve a model's understanding of
complex observations. However, the appropriate fusion
strategy depends on the structure of the inputs, the
target task, and the consequences of prediction errors.

Healthcare emphasizes clinically meaningful predictions,
privacy, calibration, and external validation. Autonomous
driving prioritizes spatial alignment, latency, and
reliability under sensor failure. Robotics requires
perception that remains consistent with physical interaction
and action, while emotion recognition must account for
contextual ambiguity and variation across speakers.
Remote sensing emphasizes cross-sensor registration and
generalization across geographic and environmental
conditions.

These differences indicate that multimodal fusion should
not be evaluated through a single architecture ranking
or a single task metric. A method that performs well in
one application may not be appropriate for another because
the available modalities, data quality, evaluation protocols,
and operational constraints differ substantially.

MULTIFUSION-7 provides a common framework for organizing
these application-specific choices. By characterizing fusion
stage, fusion mechanism, modality, learning paradigm, data
condition, backbone architecture, and application domain
together, the taxonomy supports a more systematic comparison
of multimodal methods across research areas. This perspective
also helps identify recurring challenges, including cross-modal
alignment, missing and noisy inputs, computational efficiency,
interpretability, and generalization, which motivate the
research directions discussed in the following section.

\section{Future Research Directions}
\label{sec:future}

Despite substantial progress in multimodal fusion, several
fundamental challenges remain unresolved. Existing architectures
can exploit complementary information across modalities, but
their performance may deteriorate when inputs are incomplete,
noisy, misaligned, or drawn from distributions that differ from
the training data. In addition, the increasing complexity of
fusion models creates challenges involving computational cost,
interpretability, privacy, and adaptation to changing environments.
Recent surveys have identified these issues as central obstacles
to developing reliable and generalizable multimodal learning systems
\citep{zong2025selfsupervised,wu2026missingmodality,
zhang2026lowquality,li2026alignmentfusion}.

The next stage of research should therefore move beyond optimizing
average predictive performance on fixed benchmarks. Greater
attention is needed to the conditions under which information
from different modalities is complementary, how uncertainty
should influence fusion, how models can learn from unlabeled
and incomplete data, and how their behavior can be validated
under realistic operating conditions. These challenges are
interdependent: improving alignment may increase fusion quality,
but a more complex alignment mechanism may increase computational
cost; increasing model capacity may improve representation
learning, but it may also complicate interpretability and
deployment.

Table~\ref{tab:future} summarizes seven research directions
that follow from these challenges. The proposed directions
extend the MULTIFUSION-7 perspective by connecting architectural
design, learning paradigms, data conditions, and deployment
requirements with specific research questions and measurable
outcomes.

\subsection{Unified Multimodal Representations}
\label{subsec:future_unified}

A major research direction is the development of representations
that preserve the complementary information of heterogeneous
modalities while supporting consistent cross-modal interaction.
Visual, textual, acoustic, and sensor representations differ
in their dimensionality, statistical structure, and semantic
organization. Directly projecting these representations into
a shared space may facilitate interaction, but it does not
guarantee that their semantic relationships are correctly
captured or that useful modality-specific information is retained
\citep{baltrusaitis2019survey,li2026alignmentfusion}.

Future research should investigate architectures that combine
shared representations with modality-specific components.
Shared components can support cross-modal retrieval, transfer,
and semantic correspondence, while modality-specific components
can preserve information that is meaningful only within a
particular source. Such designs may reduce the risk of losing
fine-grained information when heterogeneous inputs are forced
into a single representation space.

Recent work on unified multimodal large language models also
examines architectures, learning objectives, alignment methods,
and representation strategies for integrating different modalities
\citep{ma2026unifiedmllm}. Although these developments provide
useful architectural insights, the objective of unified
representation learning extends beyond the adoption of a
particular foundation model. For general deep learning fusion,
an important question is whether representations learned from
one set of modalities can transfer to new combinations,
tasks, or environments without requiring complete retraining.

Several research questions remain open:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item How can shared representations preserve modality-specific
    information while enabling meaningful cross-modal interaction?

    \item How can models align modalities when paired examples
    are sparse, incomplete, or temporally misaligned?

    \item Can a representation learned from one modality combination
    generalize to a different combination at inference time?

    \item How can the modality gap be measured independently of
    downstream task accuracy?

    \item How can unified representations remain computationally
    efficient as the number of modalities and input dimensions grows?
\end{enumerate}

These questions should be evaluated through cross-modal retrieval,
transfer learning, modality-ablation studies, and tests involving
unseen modality combinations. Improvements should be assessed
against modality-specific baselines and established fusion
architectures, rather than inferred from the existence of a
shared embedding space alone. The long-term objective is to
develop transferable multimodal representations that support
reliable integration without assuming that all modalities
are interchangeable.


\subsection{Efficient and Scalable Fusion}
\label{subsec:future_efficiency}

Computational efficiency is an important limitation of increasingly
complex fusion architectures. Attention-based models can incur
substantial computational and memory costs when processing
long sequences or large numbers of visual, audio, and sensor
tokens. The cost of a multimodal system also includes modality-
specific encoders, projection layers, cross-modal interaction,
and any generative components used during inference.

~\citep{jin2025efficientmllm} review efficiency
strategies for multimodal large language models, highlighting
the computational overhead associated with modality encoding,
cross-modal connectors, and large visual-token sequences.
Although their focus is on MLLMs, the underlying efficiency
considerations are also relevant to more general fusion systems.

Potential research directions include sparse or linear attention,
token pruning, low-rank projections, parameter sharing, knowledge
distillation, and conditional computation. Linear attention
methods seek to reduce the cost of processing long sequences,
although their effectiveness depends on the approximation,
architecture, and data characteristics. Token reduction can
decrease computational requirements but may discard small
objects, rare events, or fine-grained information important
to the task.

Efficiency should therefore be studied as a constrained
optimization problem rather than as an isolated reduction
in model size. A practical objective can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\theta}\;
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}(\theta)
    + \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}\,
      \mathcal{C}(\theta),
    \label{eq:future_efficiency}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}$ measures task-specific
prediction error, $\mathcal{C}(\theta)$ represents a selected
computational cost, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}$ controls
the trade-off between predictive quality and resource use.
The cost term may represent latency, memory, FLOPs, or a
combination of these quantities.

Future studies should compare methods under matched hardware,
input sizes, batch sizes, and performance requirements.
Reporting only parameter count or FLOPs is insufficient
because these measures do not necessarily predict real-world
latency or energy consumption. In addition, efficiency should
be evaluated under varying numbers of modalities and input
lengths to establish whether the method scales beyond a
single benchmark configuration.

A key research objective is to identify the smallest and
fastest fusion architecture that meets a clearly specified
performance and reliability requirement. This is particularly
important for autonomous driving, robotics, mobile systems,
and other applications where computational resources and
response time are constrained.


\subsection{Continual Multimodal Learning}
\label{subsec:future_continual}

Conventional fusion models are often trained under the assumption
that the training distribution remains representative of future
observations. In practice, sensor characteristics, environments,
tasks, and user requirements may change over time. Continual
multimodal learning aims to adapt models to new information
while retaining previously acquired capabilities.

A principal challenge is catastrophic forgetting, in which
adaptation to new tasks or distributions degrades performance
on previously learned tasks. Multimodal systems face additional
difficulties because different modalities may evolve at different
rates, become available at different times, or contribute
unequally to optimization.

~\citep{yu2026mmcl} survey recent advances in
multimodal continual learning and organize approaches into
regularization-based, architecture-based, replay-based, and
prompt-based methods. Their analysis highlights the interaction
between forgetting, modality imbalance, computational cost,
and preservation of pretrained multimodal capabilities.

Future research should investigate modality-aware memory,
selective replay, parameter-efficient adaptation, and regularization
that preserves useful cross-modal relationships. A system
may need to adapt its visual encoder to a new environment
while retaining language--vision alignment, or learn a new
sensor configuration without degrading its ability to process
previously supported inputs.

Evaluation should measure not only performance on the most
recent task but also retention of earlier tasks. Useful measures
include average incremental performance, forgetting, backward
transfer, adaptation time, and memory requirements. Experiments
should also distinguish between changes in the task distribution,
changes in modality availability, and the introduction of
entirely new modalities.

An important open question is how to update a multimodal
model when only some modalities are available during later
training stages. Another is how to preserve cross-modal
alignment when individual encoders are adapted independently.
Addressing these questions could support lifelong multimodal
systems that evolve with their operating environments without
requiring complete retraining after every change.


\subsection{Robust and Uncertainty-Aware Fusion}
\label{subsec:future_robustness}

Reliable fusion requires more than combining the available
features. The model must also account for differences in
modality quality and uncertainty. A modality may be missing,
noisy, temporally misaligned, or present but misleading.
These conditions can affect the fused representation in
different ways and should not be treated as a single failure
category.

~\citep{wu2026missingmodality} survey methods for
learning when one or more modalities are absent, while
~\citep{zhang2026lowquality} organize research
on noisy, incomplete, imbalanced, and dynamically varying
multimodal data. Together, these studies motivate the
development of fusion systems that explicitly represent
the availability and reliability of their inputs.

One possible direction is to incorporate modality-specific
uncertainty estimates into the fusion operation. Let
$u_m(\mathbf{x}^{(m)})$ represent an estimated uncertainty
or unreliability score for modality $m$. A reliability-aware
weighting function can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_m
    =
    \frac{\exp\left(-\beta u_m\right)}
    {\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp\left(-\beta u_j\right)},
    \label{eq:future_uncertainty_weights}
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ controls the sensitivity of the weighting
function to uncertainty. This expression is illustrative:
its validity depends on whether the uncertainty scores are
comparable across modalities and appropriately calibrated.
More general approaches may model aleatoric and epistemic
uncertainty separately or estimate uncertainty jointly
with the fused prediction.

Future research should consider the following priorities:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Develop reliability estimates that distinguish
    corrupted observations from genuinely ambiguous inputs.

    \item Evaluate models under multiple missingness patterns,
    corruption levels, and combinations of sensor failures.

    \item Design fusion operators that can ignore unreliable
    inputs without discarding useful complementary evidence.

    \item Study distribution shifts in which the relationship
    between modalities changes after training.

    \item Establish calibration and worst-case evaluation
    protocols appropriate to safety-critical applications.
\end{enumerate}

A further challenge is avoiding excessive dependence on
imputed or generated representations. Reconstruction may
produce plausible information that differs from the unavailable
observation. Future systems should therefore propagate
uncertainty through the reconstruction and fusion stages
rather than treating estimated inputs as fully observed data.

Robustness should ultimately be evaluated in terms of task
performance, calibration, degradation under controlled failures,
and recovery when modalities become available again. These
measures would provide stronger evidence of deployment
reliability than performance on complete and clean inputs alone.


\subsection{Self-Supervised and Label-Efficient Fusion}
\label{subsec:future_selfsupervised}

The development of multimodal fusion systems is often constrained
by the cost of collecting aligned and manually annotated
data. This limitation is particularly important when modalities
are acquired at different sampling rates or when paired
observations are available only for a small portion of the
dataset.

Self-supervised multimodal learning provides a means of
learning from unannotated observations through objectives
such as contrastive alignment, masked prediction, and
reconstruction.~\citep{zong2025selfsupervised}
identify representation learning without labels, modality
fusion, and learning from unaligned data as central
challenges in self-supervised multimodal learning.

Future research should investigate objectives that exploit
both shared and modality-specific information without
requiring every training example to contain complete,
synchronized modality pairs. Potential approaches include
cross-modal masked modeling, temporal correspondence
prediction, pair-free alignment, and hybrid reconstruction
and contrastive objectives.

A central issue is that agreement between modalities is
not always desirable for every feature. For example, audio
and visual observations may describe the same event while
retaining distinct information about speaker identity,
spatial structure, or environmental conditions. Objectives
that enforce excessive similarity may remove such information.
Future methods should therefore distinguish semantic
correspondence from the assumption that all modality-specific
representations should become identical.

Evaluation should measure label efficiency, transfer to
downstream tasks, alignment quality, and robustness when
paired data are incomplete or unaligned. Comparisons should
also account for the amount of unlabeled data and computational
resources used during pretraining.

The long-term goal is to develop fusion systems that can
learn useful relationships from naturally occurring
multimodal observations, reducing dependence on expensive
manual annotation and strict data synchronization while
preserving information relevant to the target task.


\subsection{Explainable and Trustworthy Fusion}
\label{subsec:future_explainability}

The increasing complexity of multimodal architectures creates
challenges for understanding how individual modalities influence
a prediction. A fused model may depend on different modalities
for different observations, and the interactions between
modalities may be difficult to infer from the final output
alone. This is especially important in healthcare, autonomous
driving, and other settings in which decisions must be
auditable and errors can have significant consequences.

~\citep{giovannoni2026mxai} review multimodal
explainable artificial intelligence and identify limitations
in existing explanation practices, including the reliance
on attention-based visualizations and the limited availability
of objective or human-center evaluation. Their review
motivates explanations that capture both modality-specific
evidence and the interactions through which that evidence
affects the prediction.

Future work should distinguish at least three explanatory
questions: 
\begin{enumerate}
 \item  which modality contributed to a prediction ?
 \item  which features within that modality were influential?
 \item  how interactions between modalities changed the result?.
\end{enumerate}


However, attention weights or feature-attribution maps
should not automatically be treated as faithful explanations.
A reliable explanation should be tested for stability,
sensitivity to relevant input changes, and consistency
with the model's actual predictions. Human evaluation
may also be necessary to determine whether the explanation
is understandable and useful to domain experts.
Future benchmarks should therefore assess explanation
fidelity, stability, completeness, and practical usefulness
alongside predictive performance. They should also report
whether explanations are generated intrinsically by the
model or produced by a separate post-hoc method.
The goal is not simply to make multi-modal models more
visually interpretable, but to establish whether their
reported explanations accurately describe the contribution
of individual modalities and the interactions that support
the final decision. This would provide a stronger basis
for trust and accountability in high-impact applications.


\subsection{Federated and Privacy-Preserving Fusion}
\label{subsec:future_federated}

Multi-modal learning frequently involves sensitive data that
cannot be collected centrally, including clinical records,
personal communications, and information generated by
distributed sensors. Federated learning offers a framework
in which participating clients train models locally and
exchange model updates or other permitted information
rather than directly sharing all raw observations.

~\citep{huang2024federatedmultimodal} survey
multi-modal federated learning, including its operating
settings, fusion strategies, applications, and unresolved
challenges. Their work highlights the importance of
combining heterogeneous information while respecting
constraints on centralized data access.

Federated multimodal fusion introduces additional challenges
beyond conventional federated learning. Different clients
may possess different modalities, different quantities
of data, or different distributions. One hospital may
have imaging and clinical notes, while another may
have only structured records. Similarly, distributed
sensor systems may collect different measurements
under different environmental conditions.

Potential research directions include modality-aware
federated optimization, personalized fusion modules,
communication-efficient updates, secure aggregation,
and differential privacy. These approaches address
different aspects of the problem: secure aggregation
protects individual updates during aggregation, whereas
differential privacy provides a formal framework for
limiting the influence of individual training records.
Neither should be assumed to guarantee complete privacy
without an explicit threat model and appropriate
implementation.

Evaluation should jointly report predictive performance,
communication cost, client heterogeneity, missing-modality
behaviour, and privacy-related guarantees. Experiments
should also investigate whether the global model remains
useful when some clients have incomplete modality sets
or distributions that differ substantially from those
of the remaining participants.

An important future objective is to establish federated
fusion systems that can learn shared cross-modal
relationships without requiring raw-data centralization,
while maintaining acceptable performance under
decentralized and heterogeneous operating conditions.
Such systems could be particularly useful for collaborative
healthcare, distributed robotics, and privacy-sensitive
sensor networks.


\subsection{Research Priorities and Open Questions}
\label{subsec:future_priorities}

The seven directions described above are interdependent
and should not be treated as isolated research problems.
Unified representations depend on effective alignment;
efficient architectures must preserve task-relevant information;
continual learning must prevent forgetting of cross-modal
relationships; and robust fusion requires reliable uncertainty
estimation and evaluation under realistic data conditions.

Based on this synthesis, three priorities are particularly
important. First, future studies should establish standardized
evaluation protocols that report task performance, modality
ablation, robustness, computational cost, and generalization
under consistent experimental conditions. Second, methods
should be evaluated under realistic data availability
and quality patterns rather than assuming that every
modality is complete and reliable. Third, architectural
complexity should be justified through measurable improvements
in predictive quality, robustness, efficiency, or transfer.

A further priority is to make the evidence supporting
multimodal fusion claims more reproducible. Studies should
report their training data, modality configurations,
preprocessing procedures, evaluation protocols, and
computational requirements in sufficient detail to enable
meaningful comparison. Where possible, results should
include multiple random seeds, uncertainty estimates,
and independent evaluation settings.

Within MULTIFUSION-7, these priorities can be examined
by connecting each research contribution to the fusion
stage, fusion mechanism, modalities, learning paradigm,
data conditions, backbone architecture, and application
domain involved. This perspective provides a consistent
basis for identifying where existing approaches are
effective, which assumptions limit their applicability,
and what evidence is needed to support claims of
improved multimodal learning.

Overall, future progress will depend not only on developing
more sophisticated fusion architectures, but also on
establishing systems that are computationally practical,
robust to imperfect observations, transparent in their
behavior, and capable of adapting to changing data and
application requirements. These goals provide a research
agenda for advancing multimodal fusion from benchmark-
oriented performance toward more reliable real-world
deployment.

% Section 10: Conclusion

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This review presents a comprehensive synthesis of multimodal fusion in deep learning, examining fusion architectures, representation learning, robustness, evaluation benchmarks, and applications across healthcare, autonomous driving, robotics, emotion recognition, and remote sensing. To organize this diverse research landscape, we introduce MULTIFUSION-7, a seven-dimensional taxonomy encompassing fusion stage, fusion mechanism, modality type, learning paradigm, data condition, backbone architecture, and application domain. By integrating these complementary perspectives, the proposed framework supports a more systematic comparison of existing approaches and clarifies the relationships between architectural choices, learning objectives, and practical operating conditions. The review highlights the respective advantages and limitations of early, intermediate, late, hybrid, and adaptive fusion, together with attention-based, tensor-based, graph-based, probabilistic, contrastive, generative, and gated mechanisms. It also emphasizes the importance of joint and coordinated representation learning and identifies missing, noisy, imbalanced, and asynchronous modalities as significant challenges to reliable deployment. The analysis of benchmarks and evaluation metrics further demonstrates that predictive performance alone is insufficient to establish the effectiveness of a fusion method; robustness, computational efficiency, calibration, interpretability, and reproducibility must also be considered. Despite substantial progress, important research challenges remain in developing transferable multimodal representations, efficient architectures, continual learning strategies, uncertainty-aware fusion, label-efficient representation learning, explainable models, and privacy-preserving federated systems. Future research should prioritize rigorous evaluation under realistic data conditions, transparent experimental protocols, and consistent comparisons against appropriate unimodal and multimodal baselines. In conclusion, MULTIFUSION-7 provides a unified descriptive framework for organizing and critically examining multimodal fusion research, with the aim of supporting more reliable comparisons, identifying unresolved methodological challenges, and guiding the development of efficient, robust, and trustworthy multimodal learning systems for real-world applications.
